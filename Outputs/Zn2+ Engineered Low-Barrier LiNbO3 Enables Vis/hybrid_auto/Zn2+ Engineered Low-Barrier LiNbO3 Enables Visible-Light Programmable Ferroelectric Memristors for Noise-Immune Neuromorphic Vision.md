# ABSTRACT

Lithium niobate (LiNbO ), owing to its unique ferroelectric polarization and excellent optical properties, has shown great potential in high-performance optoelectronic integrated devices. However, the high polarization switching energy barrier makes it difficult to achieve polarization reversal under low-power visible light, severely limiting its practical applicability. Here, $Z \mathrm { n } ^ { 2 + }$ ions were doped into the $\mathrm { L i N b } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 3 }$ lattice to modulate the local lattice structure via valence-state imbalance, effectively suppressing the formation of $\mathrm { N b _ { L i } } ^ { 4 + }$ antisite defects and reducing electron-trap density. Meanwhile, the narrowed bandgap enhanced carrier excitation efficiency and improved depolarization-field screening, lowering the polarization switching energy barrier by approximately 69% and enabling polarization reversal under low-energy visible light illumination (10 mW cm−2). Accordingly, the fabricated $\mathrm { P t } / \mathrm { Z n } \mathrm { - L i N b O } _ { 3 } / \mathrm { N b } { \mathrm { : } } \mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 }$ optoelectronic bimodal memristor exhibits ultra-stable switching voltage characteristics, with a voltage coefficient of variation as low as 2.2%–3.2%; a high on/off ratio of approximately 103; 24 clearly distinguishable resistance states; retention exceeding 104 s; and excellent endurance up to 108 cycles. Under visible light stimulation, the device emulates multiple representative synaptic functions, including short-term to long-term memory (STP–LTP) transition, pairedpulse facilitation (PPF), and associative learning. Moreover, an optical reservoir computing neural network constructed from the device’s multilevel optical memory and synaptic features achieves a high recognition accuracy of 98.6% on the noisecorrupted MNIST dataset, demonstrating robustness and visual recognition capability comparable to biological systems. This study proposes a new materials design paradigm for constructing low-barrier, high-performance ferroelectric optoelectronic systems with integrated sensing, storage, and computation functionalities.

# 1 Introduction

The continued advancement of artificial intelligence and edge computing has revealed critical limitations in von Neumann architectures, where the separation of processing and mem-

ory units leads to a “memory wall” that severely constrains energy efficiency and computational throughput [1–4]. This challenge has driven extensive research into nonvolatile memory technologies with in-memory computing and neuromorphic functionalities [5–7]. Ferroelectric materials, with their intrinsic

polarization bistability and remanent charge states, have emerged as promising candidates for bridging this gap [8–10]. Among them, lithium niobate (LiNbO , LNO) demonstrates significant potential in nonvolatile memories, optoelectronic synapses, and neuromorphic computing systems due to its exceptional Curie temperature, wide bandgap, and intrinsic optoelectronic coupling characteristics [11].

In recent years, LNO-based devices have achieved significant advances in nonvolatile memory, synaptic plasticity modulation, and perception-computation integration. For instance, recent pioneering studies fabricated memristors using single-crystal LNO thin films prepared by ion slicing. $\mathbf { A } \mathbf { r } ^ { + }$ ion irradiation was employed to introduce high concentrations of oxygen vacancies, enabling electrically driven oxygen vacancy migration for both nonvolatile memory storage and the emulation of synaptic plasticity [12–15]. These studies successfully realized nonvolatile storage; however, they did not fully exploit the excellent optical responsiveness of LNO. Compared with electrical control, optically controlled nonvolatile storage offers multiple advantages. It is non-contact, exhibits minimal interference, and provides high spatial selectivity, making it particularly suitable for largearea parallel writing, information encoding, and rapid readout [16, 17]. In the field of optically controlled nonvolatile storage, researchers have proposed a nonreciprocal femtosecond laser writing technique, in which domain structures within the crystal can be selectively written or erased depending on the laser scanning direction. Alternatively, other studies have induced domain walls using high electric fields and examined their effects on the photoresponsive behavior of the device [18, 19]. Although lithium niobate thin-film memristors have demonstrated significant progress in optically controlled nonvolatile memory, they still face substantial challenges in device architecture and practical deployment. First, these devices typically rely on high-quality single-crystal thin films. Their fabrication involves complex procedures such as multi-stage ion implantation, wafer bonding, delamination, and annealing. These requirements result in high processing thresholds that hinder large-scale manufacturing and system-level integration. Second, the optical modulation of ferroelectric polarization remains largely limited to highintensity illumination. Photoinduced carrier migration under visible light can maintain the polarization state only during exposure. Once the light source is removed, the state rapidly decays to its original configuration. As a result, these devices primarily function as optical detectors or transient modulators, making it difficult to achieve true optical writing and nonvolatile storage [20, 21].

In this study, we employed a pulsed laser-magnetron sputtering co-deposition system to fabricate Zn-doped LNO (Zn-LNO) thin films. $Z \mathrm { n } ^ { 2 + }$ ions were doped into the LNO lattice to modulate the local lattice structure via valence-state imbalance, suppress the formation of $\mathrm { N b _ { L i } } ^ { 4 + }$ antisite defects, and reduce electron trap density, while also enhancing carrier excitation efficiency and improving depolarization-field screening. Firstprinciples calculations revealed that the energy required for polarization switching was reduced by approximately 69% (from 146 to 45 meV), enabling ferroelectric polarization reversal under low-power visible-light illumination [22]. The fabricated Pt/Zn-LNO/Nb:SrTiO (NSTO) optoelectronic bimodal memristor exhibited excellent performance, including ultra-stable

switching voltages (with a coefficient of variation as low as 3.2%–2.2%), a high on/off ratio of approximately 103, 24 clearly distinguishable resistance states, data retention exceeding $1 0 ^ { 4 }$ s, and endurance beyond $1 0 ^ { 8 }$ switching cycles. Moreover, the device emulated various typical optical synaptic functions, such as the transition from short-term to long-term plasticity (STP to LTP), paired-pulse facilitation (PPF), experiential learning, and Pavlovian associative learning. It also successfully realized multilevel optical storage and Morse code-based optical signal transmission and decoding, demonstrating great potential for optical wireless communication. On this basis, a noise-immune optical reservoir computing neural network was constructed, achieving a high recognition accuracy of 98.6% on a heavily noise-contaminated MNIST dataset. This study not only deepens the fundamental understanding of light-ferroelectric interactions but also provides practical material and device solutions for realizing optically controlled ferroelectric nonvolatile memory technologies.

# 2 Results and Discussion

In the human visual system, visual synapses serve as the pivotal components for neural signal transmission and processing. They play essential roles in visual perception, information processing, pattern recognition, and adaptive modulation. As shown in Figure 1a, neurons communicate through synaptic connections, enabling the brain to extract meaningful information from visual stimuli, thereby supporting various cognitive visual functions [23–27]. Constructing neuromorphic synapses with biological visual functions using ferroelectric memristors has emerged as a promising strategy to overcome the limitations of conventional machine vision. However, achieving synaptic behavior under visible light critically depends on whether optical stimulation can effectively induce polarization switching in the device (Figure 1b). Although conventional LNO-based memristors can generate photoinduced carriers under illumination, their polarization states are difficult to manipulate, which limits their capacity for optical information processing (Figure 1c). Consequently, neural networks built from such devices fall short of the functional complexity observed in biological neural systems. In this work, we demonstrate that doping $Z \mathrm { n } ^ { 2 + }$ ions into LNO lowers the ferroelectric polarization energy barrier, enabling partial polarization switching under visible light illumination. This allows the device to exhibit synaptic behavior in response to optical stimuli (Figure 1d). This characteristic holds great potential to significantly enhance the image recognition performance of convolutional neural networks constructed from such devices [28].

The pure-phase LNO thin films and Zn-LNO thin films with a ZnO doping concentration of 5 mol.% were fabricated using pulsed laser-magnetron sputtering co-deposition technology, as shown in Figure 2a. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was performed to examine the crystal structures of both films, revealing that $Z \mathrm { n } ^ { 2 + }$ doping did not alter the crystalline structure. Interestingly, the (006) characteristic peak of Zn-LNO shifts to a lower angle in the XRD pattern. During the growth process of LNO thin films, Li is prone to volatilization or deficiency, leading to the formation of lithium vacancies. To maintain charge neutrality, some $\mathrm { N b ^ { 5 + } }$ ions migrate to occupy Li sites, forming Nb antisite

defects $( \mathrm { N b } _ { \mathrm { L i } } )$ . Upon ${ \mathrm { Z n ^ { 2 + } } }$ doping, due to the similarity in ionic radii, $Z \mathrm { n } ^ { 2 + }$ ions preferentially substitute for Li+ ions in the lattice. It is worth noting that the ionic radius of $Z \mathrm { n } ^ { 2 + }$ (0.074 nm) is smaller than that of Li+ (0.076 nm). From this perspective, the replacement of the larger $\mathrm { L i ^ { + } }$ by the smaller $Z \mathrm { n } ^ { 2 + }$ would theoretically lead to a reduction in the lattice constant. However, this contradicts our experimental observations. We attribute this anomaly to the imbalance in ionic valence states. When $Z \mathrm { n } ^ { 2 + }$ ions substitute for $\mathrm { L i ^ { + } }$ ions, positively charged $\mathrm { Z n _ { L i } } ^ { + }$ defects are formed, which suppress the formation of $\mathrm { N b _ { L i } } ^ { 4 + }$ defects. The Coulomb repulsion between $\mathrm { { Z n _ { L i } } ^ { + } }$ and neighboring $\mathrm { N b _ { L i } } ^ { 4 + }$ further modulates the local lattice structure, ultimately resulting in an increased lattice constant and a shift of the XRD peak to lower angles [29, 30]. As shown in Figure 2b, to obtain detailed structural information of the samples, Rietveld refinement was conducted on the XRD patterns within the 2θ range of $2 5 ^ { \circ } - 6 5 ^ { \circ }$ , encompassing all major Bragg peaks of Zn-LNO. The refinement employed the R-3c crystal structure model, yielding a weighted profile R-factor $( \mathrm { R } _ { \mathrm { w p } } )$ of 4.5% and a goodness-of-fit $( \chi ^ { 2 } )$ of less than 1.06, indicating a satisfactory refinement result [31]. The refinement results are summarized in Table S1. It shows that the lattice constant along the c-axis of the LNO film increases significantly after doping with ZnO, which is consistent with the peak shift observed in the XRD patterns. These results indicate that $Z \mathrm { n } ^ { 2 + }$ ions are incorporated into the lattice via substitutional rather than interstitial doping. In addition, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was used to analyze the composition and chemical states of the Zn-LNO film. The full XPS spectrum of the film is shown in Figure S1a, and the scan confirms the presence of Li, Nb, O, and Zn elements in the Zn-LNO film. Figure 2c shows the Zn 2p spectrum. The fitted binding energy peaks at 1045.19 and 1022.06 eV correspond to Zn $2 \mathsf { p } _ { 1 / 2 }$ and Zn $2 { \tt p } _ { 3 / 2 } ,$ , respectively, confirming the presence of $Z \mathrm { n } ^ { 2 + }$ ions in the LNO film. Figure S1b shows the Nb 3d spectrum. The two peaks correspond to Nb $3 \mathrm { d } _ { 5 / 2 }$ and Nb $3 \mathrm { d } _ { 3 / 2 }$ , with binding energies of 206.8 and 209.5 eV, respectively. The peak separation of 2.7 eV indicates that Nb in the film exhibits the + 5 oxidation state. Figure S1c shows the Li 1s spectrum, with a binding energy peak located at approximately

54.7 $\mathrm { e V , }$ confirming that lithium exists in the +1 oxidation state in the film.

As shown in Figure 2d, the TEM image of the film reveals a thickness of approximately 85 nm. Figure 2e presents a highresolution image that clearly shows the high-quality crystalline state of the Zn-LNO film. Focusing on region I of Figure 2e, an atomic resolution image of the Zn-LNO/NSTO interface was obtained. Figure 2g shows the atomic structural model of Zn-LNO and NSTO along with the corresponding crystal planes, indicating a strong epitaxial relationship between the Zn-LNO layer and the NSTO substrate. Figure 2f,h shows the fast Fourier transform (FFT) of NSTO and Zn-LNO, further confirming the preferred orientation growth of the Zn-LNO film on the NSTO substrate. Figure 2i shows a high-resolution image of region II in Figure 2e, where the lattice fringes marked by white lines clearly reveal the lattice distortion, which is in high agreement with the conclusion of $Z \mathrm { n } ^ { 2 + }$ substitutional doping inferred from the peak shift in the XRD spectrum.

# 2.1 Optically Controlled Ferroelectric Polarization Mechanism

Figure S2a presents the ferroelectric properties of the thinfilm characterized by piezoresponse force microscopy (PFM). Applying a bias voltage of −8 V induced downward polarization in the Zn-LNO film, whereas a +8 V bias reversed the polarization direction to upward. These results demonstrate that different bias voltages can effectively control the polarization direction of ferroelectric domains. Figure 3a shows the corresponding PFM phase and amplitude hysteresis loops, which exhibit clear polarization switching behavior. The coercive voltages are approximately 1.9 and −2.3 V, and the loops display excellent ferroelectric switching characteristics. In addition, to verify the tunability of ferroelectric polarization under different voltages, PFM measurements were carried out at applied voltages of 2, 4, 6, and 8 V. As shown in Figure S2b,c, with increasing applied voltage, the PFM images

exhibit a stepwise phase evolution. Figure 3b presents the ferroelectric hysteresis loops of the device. The pink curve represents the original hysteresis loop. Under 405 nm laser illumination at $1 0 \mathrm { \ m W \ c m ^ { - 2 } }$ , the hysteresis loop (blue) shows notable changes: the maximum polarization $( P _ { \mathrm { m a x } } )$ increases from 2.98 to 3.58 µC $\mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ , and the remanent polarization $( P _ { \mathrm { r } } )$ rises from 0.52 to 1.4 $\mu \mathrm { C } \ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ . The purple curve corresponds to the measurement taken after the light was turned off for a period. Although the polarization decreases, it remains higher than the initial level. These results indicate that the ferroelectric polarization of the film can be modulated by light and exhibits robust nonvolatile behavior [32–34]. This differs from the previously reported light-controlled ferroelectric properties of LNO films. To further verify this phenomenon, PFM tests were performed on the films following exposure to light for different durations [35, 36]. As shown in Figure S3, $\mathbf { a } \pm 5 \mathrm { V }$ bias voltage was applied via the probe tip to polarize the film into downward (left) and upward (right) polarization states, respectively. The entire region

was then exposed to three successive light pulses (wavelength: 405 nm, intensity: 250 mW $\mathrm { c m } ^ { - 2 }$ , pulse width: 300 s). After each illumination, the same area was scanned in a dark environment. With increasing cumulative exposure time, the region initially in the downward polarization state gradually switched to an upward polarization state, while the region originally in the upward state exhibited negligible change. These results further confirm that light can modulate the polarization state of the film and that the photoinduced polarization exhibits nonvolatile behavior. Subsequently, the stability of the sample was evaluated using a ferroelectric tester. Figure S4 shows the relationship between remanent polarization intensity and the number of switching cycles under illumination and in the dark: after $1 0 ^ { 8 }$ polarization reversals, the remanent polarization intensity shows no significant degradation, demonstrating the excellent fatigue resistance of the device. The combination of this nonvolatile characteristic and optoelectronic dynamic modulation enables the Zn-LNO film to directly emulate the long-term plasticity of biological

synapses, providing a feasible approach for constructing highly biomimetic and ultra-stable optical artificial synaptic devices. The ferroelectric polarization switching mechanism of LNO has been widely confirmed. It involves the displacement of $\mathrm { L i ^ { + } }$ and $\mathrm { N b ^ { 5 + } }$ ions along the c-axis, where $\mathrm { L i ^ { + } }$ ions move away from the shared faces of the oxygen octahedra and $\mathrm { N b ^ { 5 + } }$ ions shift from the centers of the octahedra, forming an electric dipole along the c-axis [37]. To elucidate the mechanism underlying the non-volatility of light-controlled ferroelectric polarization in Zn-LNO films, first-principles calculations were performed, as shown in Figure 3c. The results indicate that the introduction of $Z \mathrm { n } ^ { 2 + }$ ions significantly reduces the energy barrier for ferroelectric polarization switching, from 146 to 45 meV [38, 39]. This effect can be attributed to the following mechanism: in the LNO lattice, $\mathrm { N b _ { L i } } ^ { 4 + }$ defects formed during high-temperature deposition usually pair with four Li vacancies $\left( 4 \mathrm { V _ { L i } } ^ { - } \right)$ to achieve charge compensation, thus remaining energetically stable. When $Z \mathrm { n } ^ { 2 + }$

ions are incorporated into the lattice, they preferentially occupy Li sites and fill Li vacancies, forming positively charged $\mathrm { Z n _ { L i } } ^ { + }$ defects, which simultaneously suppress the formation of $\mathrm { N b _ { L i } } ^ { 4 + }$ defects. Through this defect regulation, $Z \mathrm { n } ^ { 2 + }$ doping effectively reduces the density of electron traps and decreases electron capture probability, thereby enhancing carrier concentration and mobility.

To further understand the impact of $Z \mathrm { n } ^ { 2 + }$ doping on the optical and electrical properties of LNO, we calculated the band structures before and after doping, as shown in Figure S5a. The bandgap of the undoped LNO is 3.41 eV, slightly lower than the experimentally measured 3.78 eV, primarily due to the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) typically underestimating the bandgap. Figure S5b shows the band structure of Zn-LNO. Compared with undoped LNO, both the conduction band and valence band of Zn-LNO shift downward to varying degrees, while the

bandgap narrows to 3.32 eV. The reduced bandgap facilitates thermal excitation of carriers, thereby increasing the density of photogenerated carriers; the increased carrier concentration can screen the local depolarization field, synergistically lowering the energy barrier for polarization switching.

To better understand the mechanism of light-induced nonvolatile polarization switching, a simplified model was constructed [40]. Figure 3d illustrates a schematic representation of $Z \mathrm { n } ^ { 2 + }$ ions substituting for $\mathrm { L i ^ { + } }$ ions within the lithium niobate unit cell. Assuming that the Zn-LNO ferroelectric film exhibits two distinct polarization states-upward and downward-with a net zero polarization under equilibrium conditions, Figure 3e demonstrates that the application of an external electric field induces alignment of the ferroelectric domains along the field direction. Domains aligned with the field expand, while those in the opposite direction contract. When the applied voltage exceeds the coercive threshold, domain switching occurs. Upon removal of the external field, remanent polarization persists, maintaining the ferroelectric state in the direction of the previously applied field. As shown in Figure 3f, under illumination, the material absorbs photons, leading to the excitation of electrons from the valence band to the conduction band and the generation of electron–hole pairs via the photoelectric effect. The resulting built-in electric field, assumed to point upward due to charge separation, promotes the growth of upward-oriented domains while suppressing downward ones. Due to $Z \mathrm { n } ^ { 2 + }$ incorporation, the energy barrier for ferroelectric switching is reduced by approximately 69%, rendering the photogenerated internal field sufficiently strong to reverse the downward domains. After the light is removed, the built-in field vanishes; however, the newly oriented domains are retained, yielding a net upward polarization-indicative of a nonvolatile polarization state. In contrast, as shown in Figure S6a,b, in undoped LNO, the high energy barrier prevents the photogenerated internal field from reversing the downward domains, and the polarization state rapidly relaxes to its initial configuration once the illumination ceases. Therefore, the incorporation of $Z \mathrm { n } ^ { 2 + }$ plays a critical role in enabling light-induced nonvolatile polarization switching by significantly lowering the energy barrier for domain reversal.

# 2.2 Electrical Performance Measurement of Devices

Biological neurons form memories by dynamically adjusting the strength of synaptic connections. Under the drive of action potentials, presynaptic neurons release neurotransmitters that act on receptors on the postsynaptic membrane, generating excitatory or inhibitory postsynaptic potentials. The polarization state of ferroelectric materials can induce stable and controllable resistance state changes, directly emulating this synaptic regulation process [41]. Figure 4a shows a schematic of an artificial synapse based on a Pt/Zn-LNO/NSTO memristor, mimicking a biological synapse connected to a neuron. To assess the periodic performance of the Zn-LNO synaptic device, 100 consecutive I–V curves are shown in Figure 4b. The device exhibits typical bipolar nonvolatile resistive switching behavior. Under a forward voltage sweep (0 to 1.6 V to 0), the device switches from a high resistance state (HRS) to a low resistance state (LRS), and reverses back under a reverse sweep (0

to −2 V to 0). No noticeable fluctuations are observed, indicating excellent stability. The inset shows the I–V curve of a previously reported pristine LNO memristor, indicating that $Z \mathrm { n } ^ { 2 + }$ doping increases the device on/off ratio by approximately 50-fold. This improvement is attributed to the Coulomb repulsion between $\mathrm { Z n _ { L i } } ^ { + }$ and neighboring $\mathrm { N b _ { L i } } ^ { 4 + }$ ions, which effectively relieves local lattice stress and increases the displacement of central ions within the $\mathrm { N b O } _ { 6 }$ octahedra. The enhanced polarization improves the modulation of the internal electric field, enabling more effective control of the interfacial barrier and significantly increasing the resistance contrast between HRS and LRS. Figure 4c presents the distribution of switching voltages after 100 cycles and the corresponding Gaussian fit. The fitting parameters-coefficient of variation $( C _ { \mathrm { V } } )$ , standard deviation (σ), and mean (µ) are extracted using the relation $C _ { \mathrm { V } } = \sigma / \mu$ . The $C _ { \mathrm { V } }$ values for the set and reset voltages are 2.2% and 2.5% [42], respectively. As summarized in Table S2, these values compare favorably with previously reported devices, confirming excellent switching stability. To evaluate endurance, Figure 4d shows pulsed cycling results. The device maintains stable operation over $1 0 ^ { 8 }$ cycles without degradation. Figure 4e displays cumulative probability distributions of eight representative $I { - } V$ configurations selected from the 100 cycles, demonstrating good uniformity and consistency. Figure 4f illustrates the retention of $2 ^ { 4 }$ distinct resistance states, all remaining stable for up to 104 s, indicating multilevel conductance tunability and long-term nonvolatile memory performance. The excellent stability primarily originates from $Z \mathrm { n } ^ { 2 + }$ doping, which suppresses the formation of $\mathrm { N b _ { L i } } ^ { 4 + }$ antisite defects and optimizes the lattice structure. This leads to a more uniform electric field distribution during polarization switching, significantly reducing voltage fluctuations and ensuring stable and reproducible switching behavior. As a result, the device maintains stable and reproducible switching voltages over repeated cycles, significantly improving reliability and uniformity. Additionally, the device’s conductance tunability under different pulse parameters (amplitude, pulse width, and interval) was investigated, with the corresponding results shown in Figure S7a–c. Due to the reversal of ferroelectric polarization, the device’s conductance gradually adjusts with changes in the amplitude, pulse width, and interval of the applied pulses. In addition, the gradual tunability of the conductance provides the foundation for long-term potentiation (LTP) and long-term depression (LTD) functions. To investigate synaptic long-term plasticity, 40 positive voltage pulses (amplitude of 2.75 V, pulse width of 0.25 µs, and interval of 0.25 µs) were applied, followed by 40 negative voltage pulses. The results showed that the device’s conductance increased from 3.29 to 6.47 µS and then returned to the initial value, confirming the LTP and LTD processes. To explore the device’s application in neuromorphic computing, 10 sets of LTP/LTD were applied. Figure 4g shows that the device exhibits good repeatability, indicating that it can be used for the training and recognition of artificial neural networks. In addition to the aforementioned simulated synaptic plasticity learning rules, as shown in Figure S8a–c, it also simulated three typical learning rules: spike-rate-dependent plasticity (SRDP), spike-duration-dependent plasticity (SDDP), and spikeamplitude-dependent plasticity (SADP). Synaptic plasticity can further be classified into short-term memory (STM) and longterm memory (LTM) based on the relaxation time. As shown in Figure S9, the relationship between the relaxation time (τ) and the number of pulses is demonstrated. With the increase in the number of stimulation pulses, the relaxation time (τ)

also increases, which is highly similar to the forgetting behavior observed in biological synapses.

# 2.3 Optical Performance Measurement of Devices

Figure 5a investigates the effect of different light wavelengths on excitatory postsynaptic current (EPSC). The results show that as the wavelength decreases from 650 to 405 nm, the EPSC value induced by the first light pulse increases from 0.65 to 0.95 µA, while the EPSC current value induced by the tenth light pulse increases from 1.06 to 2.54 µA. The intensity-dependent plasticity of synaptic behavior was investigated by adjusting the light intensity. Figure 5c shows the photoelectric current response under four different light intensities. At low light intensity, the photoelectric current rapidly decays to a lower level after the light is turned off, resembling short-term memory (STM) in biological synapses. In addition to intensity-dependent plasticity, the light exposure time-dependent plasticity was also investigated. As shown in Figure 5d, with the increase in light exposure duration, the photoelectric current gradually increases, and the decay process is prolonged, indicating an increase in synaptic weight.

Similarly, Figure 5e shows that with an increasing number of light pulses, the photoelectric current response is enhanced, and the decay process slows down. Figure 5f shows that at high light exposure frequency, the EPSC value of the device is larger, and the decay is slower, which is consistent with the phenomenon in biological systems where repeated stimulation within a short time enhances learning and memory. Therefore, the device can simulate the STM and LTM characteristics of synapses by adjusting the light intensity, duration, and frequency, demonstrating its ability to mimic the response of biological synapses to external stimuli. In addition, the potential of the device for optical wireless communication methods in humanmachine interfaces were validated, using the international Morse code for Arabic numerals (0–9) as the purple light pulse pattern [47]. In Figure S10, distinct EPSC responses are observed for each Arabic numeral, indicating the potential of our device to achieve

optical fidelity in the future. To further characterize the device’s nonvolatile multi-level storage properties [48], Figure $5 \mathrm { g }$ shows eight distinguishable nonvolatile resistance states obtained by applying eight consecutive optical pulses (wavelength: 405 nm, pulse width: 1 s, interval: 300 s), demonstrating the device’s multi-level optical storage capability. The successful emulation of synaptic plasticity originates from the coupling effect between photoexcited carriers and polarization states in the Zn-LNO film, enabling the modulation of spontaneous polarization and inducing stable and controllable resistive state changes.

From a biological perspective, learning behavior typically involves the processes of learning, forgetting, and relearning. When acquiring new information, forgetting generally occurs over time. # 2.4 Neuromorphic Visual Simulation Recognition

Traditional feedforward neural networks suffer from issues such as vanishing and exploding gradients, whereas reservoir computing (RC) networks can effectively address these problems. In memristor-based reservoir computing networks, the internal physical properties of the devices determine the connections between virtual nodes, allowing complex computations to be transformed into changes in the device’s internal states. A simple readout layer is sufficient for data classification, significantly reducing the training cost of the network [51–54]. Given the nonlinear optical response and multi-level storage characteristics of the Zn-LNO optoelectronic synaptic device, which are beneficial for the application of RC systems in image recognition,

we constructed an optical reservoir computing network and conducted image recognition tests. Figure 6a shows the processing flow and overall architecture of the optical reservoir computing system. To evaluate the network’s resistance to interference in image recognition under complex environments, Gaussian noise of varying intensities was added to the MNIST dataset to simulate harsh conditions such as fog and sandstorms. During the operation of the network, we first binarized and scaled the dataset, then reassembled it into 40 × 4 pixel images. Next, each row of 4 pixels was converted into 4 optical pulse sequences. By applying different optical encoding pulses, pixel information was transformed into the conductance state of the memristor and applied to the storage layer. Finally, classification was performed through a simple readout layer. Figure 6b shows the conductance states of the memristor under different encodings. A total of 16 optical pulse sequences from “0000” to “1111” were tested for their current responses, with complete results provided in Figure S10. The results indicate that memristors with different conductance states exhibited good separability, which is a necessary condition for building an efficient RC network. Figure S13 presents the photocurrent responses of the device under different encoding more clearly. To ensure the stable operation of the RC network, as shown in Figure S14, we tested the optoelectrical current responses under the same optical pulse sequence five times. The results demonstrate that the device exhibits high repeatability. Subsequently, we further evaluated the uniformity among different devices. Four devices randomly selected under identical fabrication conditions were tested for reservoir computing encoding. As shown in Figure S15a,b, under the same encoding conditions, these devices exhibit consistent and stable state evolution; under different encoding conditions, they also demonstrate good repeatability and reliability. Figure 6c shows the MNIST dataset images under different levels of Gaussian noise. # 3 Conclusion

In summary, we have successfully fabricated high-quality Zndoped $\mathrm { L i N b } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 3 }$ thin films using a pulsed laser-magnetron sputtering co-deposition system and demonstrated Pt/Zn-LNO/NSTO optoelectronic bimodal memristors with outstanding performance. $Z \mathrm { n } ^ { 2 + }$ doping effectively modulated the local lattice structure, suppressed $\mathrm { N b _ { L i } } ^ { 4 + }$ antisite defects, and reduced electron

-trap density, while bandgap narrowing enhanced carrier excitation and improved depolarization-field screening. Both experimental results and first-principles calculations revealed that the ferroelectric polarization switching barrier was reduced by approximately 69%, enabling visible-light-driven polarization reversal. This improvement enabled our devices to exhibit exceptionally stable switching voltages (with voltage coefficients of variation as low as 2.2%–2.5%), a high on/off ratio of approximately 103, 24 stable resistive states, long data retention exceeding $1 0 ^ { 4 }$ s, and outstanding endurance over 108 switching cycles. Moreover, the devices successfully emulated multiple representative synaptic plasticity behaviors under optical stimulation. On this basis, we constructed a noise-resilient optical reservoir computing neural network, achieving a recognition accuracy of 98.6% on the MNIST dataset under high-noise conditions. The doping engineering strategy presented in this work provides a feasible approach for modulating optical responses in ferroelectric material systems and shows great promise for promoting the

application of novel optoelectronic integrated devices in cuttingedge fields such as neuromorphic computing and intelligent sensing.

# 4 Experimental Methods

# 4.1 Device Fabrication

The ferroelectric optical memristors analyzed in this study were fabricated using pulsed laser-magnetron sputtering co-deposition technology. First, NSTO(111) single crystals were used as substrates. The substrates were immersed in acetone and ethanol at room-temperature for 5 min, followed by ultrasonic cleaning in deionized water for 10 min, and then dried with pure nitrogen gas. Prior to thin-film deposition, the vacuum in the chamber was maintained at $2 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 }$ Pa. During the deposition of the Zn-LNO thin-film, the deposition temperature was set at $7 0 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ , with an

oxygen pressure of 7.6 Pa and a deposition thickness of 85 nm. The distance between the sample and the target was maintained at 5.5 cm, and the rotation speed of the sample and target was set at $5 ^ { \circ } \ \mathbf { s } ^ { - 1 }$ . The laser fluence was maintained at 230 J cm−2 with a repetition rate of 3 Hz. After deposition, the device was kept in place and annealed at 600◦C for 60 min, followed by cooling in an oxygen atmosphere at $8 \times 1 0 ^ { 4 }$ Pa. Finally, the top platinum electrode with a diameter of 100 µm was fabricated using DC magnetron sputtering in an argon atmosphere at $3 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 } \mathrm { P a }$ .

# 4.2 Characterization

The XRD spectra of the devices were measured using a Bruker D8 Advance. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was conducted using a Fei Tecnai G2 F20 ST FE-TEM. Piezoelectric force microscopy (PFM) measurements were performed using a commercial AFM system (Cipher-S, Asylum Research, GAI-SA). The ferroelectric properties of the devices were measured with a TZFE-200 F ferroelectric tester. The photoelectric characteristics of the memristors were characterized using a Keithley 4200 semiconductor parameter analyzer, Keysight B1500A parameter analyzer, Keithley 2400 instrument, RIGOL DG 5072 arbitrary waveform generator, and RIGOL MSO 4034 digital oscilloscope.

# 4.3 DFT Calculations

The Vienna Ab-initio Simulation Package (VASP) code was used to calculate by employing the density functional theory (DFT) within the projector-augmented-wave (PAW) method [55, 56]. The generalized gradient approximation (GGA) with Perdew–Burke– Ernzerhof (PBE) parametrization was performed [57]. The cutoff energy of the plane-wave basis was set at 500 eV. The conjugate gradient method was adopted during geometry optimization, and the criterion of energy convergence was 10−5 eV/cell. For kspace summation, a 4 × 4 × 4 Monkhorst–Pack k-point grid was employed for the $2 \times 2 \times 2$ supercell of pristine and Zn-doped LiNbO . The smearing width of the partial occupancies, which were determined using the Methfessel–Paxton smearing scheme, was 0.05 eV [58]. The climbing image nudged elastic band (CI-NEB) method with five images was employed to calculate the energy barrier for polarization switching [59, 60]. The images are relaxed until the maximum force per atom was less than 0.03 eV Å−1 .
