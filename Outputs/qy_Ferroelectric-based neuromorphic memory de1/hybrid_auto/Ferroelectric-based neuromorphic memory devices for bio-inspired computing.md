# Ferroelectric-based neuromorphic memory devices for bio-inspired computing

Yihan Liu1,4, Weiyi Tang1,4, Jinhua Zeng  2,4, Chongyang Bai2 , Keji Zhou1 , Xumeng Zhang  1 , Qi Liu  1 , Zhangcheng Huang  1,5 , Guangjian Wu  1,5 & Jianlu Wang  1,3,5

# Abstract

The growing gap between the rapidly increasing demand for computing power and the slowing improvements in computing speed is becoming more noticeable within the von Neumann architecture. Ferroelectric materials, such as hafnium-based ferroelectrics and two-dimensional (2D) van der Waals ferroelectrics, are promising for neuromorphic computing because the partial ferroelectric domain switching behaviour can emulate the temporal dynamics of biological neurons and synapses. Because ferroelectric devices are driven by electric felds, their writing energy is much lower than that of other efcient materials used for memory, such as phase change memory and resistive random-access memory. In this Review, we discuss the advances in ferroelectric neuromorphic devices and arrays, and their in-sensor applications. We summarize the device structure and principles of ferroelectric synaptic devices and neuronal circuits. Furthermore, we emphasize the key role of ferroelectric devices in building efcient and scalable synapse and neuron arrays, including topologies of various structures, the potential for more physical domain computing and high-density 3D integration. Finally, we discuss ferroelectric materials as a key component in supporting workloads that are unattainable using complementary metal-oxide semiconductor (CMOS)-based memory technology.

# Key points

• Ferroelectric polarization switching is related to a device’s domain structure and is primarily driven by applied electric fields explained by the Kolmogorov–Avrami–Ishibashi model, the nucleation-limited switching model and other models.   
• The multi-state programmability and non-volatility of ferroelectric polarization are the foundation for realizing ferroelectric artificial synapses and neurons, and the availability of both two-terminal and three-terminal device structures enables flexible and compact designs for synaptic and neuronal implementations.   
• Ferroelectric synapse arrays feature diverse topologies, including two-dimensional (2D) or 3D integration and two-terminal or three-terminal device configurations, ofering complementary metal-oxide semiconductor (CMOS) compatibility for scalable neuromorphic systems.   
• In-sensor computing using ferroelectric neuromorphic devices can improve image preprocessing by suppressing background noise, enhancing contrast and realizing high-level processing functions such as edge detection and pattern classification.

# Introduction

Computing capability has dramatically increased in processing speed and energy efficiency using conventional von Neumann architecture since the 1970s, with the number of transistors per processor increasing from 2,300 in Intel’s 4004 processor1 in 1971 to more than 10 billion in modern GPUs as of 2025 (ref. 2), largely owing to the decrease in size of modern transistors. Nevertheless, as complementary metal-oxide semiconductor (CMOS)-based transistors decrease in size to only several nanometres3 , the leakage current worsens. Meanwhile, the speed of data access from memory lags substantially behind the computing speed of processing units. This issue, known as the memory wall, further limits computer performance based on the von Neumann architecture. The Internet of Things brings about an exponential growth of data and the advent of artificial intelligence has imposed higher demands on energy efficiency and latency. To maintain advances such as artificial intelligence and the Internet of Things devices, computers need advanced memory architectures that can break the memory wall.

The bio-inspired neuromorphic computing architecture has promised energy-efficient computing since the unprecedented success of brain-inspired algorithms in machine learning in 2012 (refs. 4,5). Human brains are capable of complicated tasks with co-location of memory and processing units, robustness to local errors and parallel computing properties6–10. They require only about 20 W of power11, which is much more efficient than state-of-the-art processors that achieve comparable performance at much higher power levels, typically 250 W for the GeForce Titan X GPU12. Neurons and synapses are the fundamental structural and functional units of the nervous system, including the human brain. A neuron consists of dendrites, a soma and an axon, performing input non-linear transformations13 (Fig. 1a). Synapses modulate the information transmission based on the stored weights14. Typical synaptic functions include excitation and inhibition, short-term plasticity, long-term plasticity and synaptic reverberation.

Typical neuronal functions include integrate and fire, periodic action potential and spike number adaptation15.

Based on the concepts of synapses, neurons and the biological neural systems, artificial neural networks and their subgroups — convolutional neural networks or recurrent neural networks — have been developed to handle various tasks, including image recognition and natural language processing16,17. A third generation of artificial neural networks known as spike neural networks has also been developed, which only process spike trains, a temporal sequence of discrete electrical pulses encoding information through timing, frequency and patterns16,18,19. This process is most similar to the operations of the human brain. Also, large-scale neuromorphic computers have been demonstrated, such as Intel’s Loihi and IBM’s TrueNorth that have more than 5 million transistors20,21. Generally, CMOS-based neuromorphic circuits require so many transistors because tens of transistors and capacitors are required to emulate a single neuron. Furthermore, synapses in CMOS chips based on volatile random-access memory suffer from high energy consumption22 of up to ~10 nJ per operation.

Neuromorphic computing requires the study of new material. Non-volatile materials induced by electrochemical defect migration, phase transitions or spin injection (see Supplementary Information 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1) can store physical states without a constant power supply, making them attractive for more energy-efficient memory applications23–26. Ferroelectric materials have electrically tunable, non-volatile polarization that mimics biological synapses. They show great promise for neuromorphic computing, especially with the rapid advancements in hafnium-based ferroelectrics and emerging two-dimensional (2D) van der Waals ferroelectrics27–29 (Fig. 1b; see Supplementary Information 2). Such developments have sparked a renewed surge of interest in ferroelectric physics and device research.

In this Review, we examine ferroelectric-based neuromorphic devices, including the available materials, ferroelectric switching dynamics, arrays and applications. First, we introduce the models of domain switching dynamics and their relationship to device performance and then summarize efforts to realize synaptic devices in two-terminal and three-terminal devices. The following section discusses neuron models and their circuit implementation. Then, we introduce ferroelectric neuromorphic device arrays and their applications for in-memory and in-sensor computing. Finally, an outlook detailing the remaining challenges to realizing neuromorphic memory devices is also provided.

# Ferroelectric polarization switching

In ferroelectric materials, a spatial region with the same spontaneous polarization is called a domain and their boundaries are domain walls. Domain switching kinetics has a crucial impact on the performance of a ferroelectric device. Several models have been proposed to describe it (see Supplementary Information 3, Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 1). Merz’s law30, as an empirical law, shows an exponential relationship between the switching time and the applied electric field, but is unable to reveal the microscopic processes occurring during polarization reversal. The Kolmogorov–Avrami–Ishibashi model31–33 describes ferroelectric domain switching by domain wall propagation and domain coalescence. The key idea of the Kolmogorov–Avrami– Ishibashi model is that ferroelectric domains are formed at a fixed time and the switching dynamic is dominated by domain wall motion.

# Ferroelectric-based synapse devices

Synaptic weight is the connection strength between the presynaptic neuron and the postsynaptic neuron, reflecting the efficiency of signal transmission. It can be changed by synaptic activity, called synaptic plasticity, including short-term potentiation, short-term depression, long-term potentiation (LTP) and long-term depression (LTD). Short-term plasticity is used for the recognition and processing of external signals and long-term plasticity is related to memory and learning37. Considering that information in the biological nervous system is transmitted through synapses, synaptic devices with excellent synaptic function are an essential part of hardware implementation in neuromorphic computing, especially the ideal LTP and LTD curve characteristic (see Supplementary Information 4).

Ferroelectric materials possess unique non-volatile polarization properties, and their polarization level can be continuously tuned by an external electric field, similar to the working mechanism of biological synapses. Therefore, artificial synaptic devices based on ferroelectric materials have received extensive attention38. Although their synaptic functions rely on ferroelectric polarization, the specific physical mechanisms are worth discussing.

# Two-terminal ferroelectric synapses

Metal–ferroelectric–metal (or metal–ferroelectric–semiconductor) is the typical structure of two-terminal ferroelectric artificial synapses, in which the ferroelectric layer acts as a memory medium. In addition to effectively simulating biological synaptic functions, the two-terminal ferroelectric artificial synapse also has the advantage of a simple structure. For two-terminal ferroelectric artificial synapses, the polarization direction of the intermediate ferroelectric can be adjusted by the applied electric field, thus changing the conductivity state of the device, which can be used as the synaptic weight39. As shown in Fig. 2a, when a presynaptic signal is applied to one electrode, a corresponding change in the conductance is generated and the synaptic weights can be read from the other electrode, allowing for the transmission of the spike signal from the presynaptic neuron to the postsynaptic neuron. The physical mechanism responsible for switchable resistive behaviour in two-terminal ferroelectric artificial synapses is attributed mainly to the modulation of contact barrier height (Fig. 2b), contact barrier width (Fig. 2c) and migration of oxygen vacancies40–43 (Fig. 2d). Notably, realistic conductance modulation is not just determined by one mechanism. Therefore, the classification simply refers to the main factors. These mechanisms modulate conductance to enable both short-term and long-term analogue synaptic plasticity in two-terminal ferroelectric synapses.

FTJs are the most extensively studied two-terminal synaptic devices. Oxygen vacancy migration. During the deposition process of complex oxide ferroelectric films, some oxygen vacancies are inevitably present, owing to the reductive deposition environment caused by insufficient oxygen pressure. As one of the most likely defects, the migration of oxygen vacancies has an important role in the resistive switching process of oxide resistive switching devices. For ferroelectric diodes, as shown in Fig. 2d, oxygen vacancies enable the formation of conductive filaments within the ferroelectric film, which can be ruptured (high-resistance state) and reformed (low-resistance state) by electrical pulses, yielding resistance switching properties55. This phenomenon is observed in the ${ \mathsf { B i } } _ { 2 } { \mathsf { F e M n O } } _ { 6 }$ -based ferroelectric synapse. By setting and resetting, the resistance changes between the high-resistance state and the low-resistance state. Under different set voltages, the device exhibited stable multilevel storage characteristics41,56. The migration of oxygen vacancies simultaneously alters the interface barrier and modulates the conductivity state of the device, and oxygen vacancies can be controlled by the doping ratio57. A resistive switching memory based on BiFeO3 (BFO) with varying samariam doping ratios shows various multiple current jumps and discrete resistance states. These current jumps occur during the resistance switching process due to electron trapping by oxygen vacancies58. These oxygen vacancies can also act as anchoring centres for domain wall growth, resulting in domain wall creep behaviour and influencing the domain switch.

Among the devices described above, the conductance of a memristor is only controlled by the applied external voltage, which is referred to as a first-order memristor15,59. In contrast, biological synapses possess an internal timing mechanism that determines their instantaneous state and subsequent synaptic plasticity. It is challenging to achieve the temporal dynamics of biological synapses with just a first-order memristor. Under the interaction of depolarization effects and charge accumulation, the device exhibits different responses to applied pulse frequencies. These two effects can be separated by different voltage schemes. High-frequency (200 Hz) voltage pulses gradually increase the voltage drop across the ferroelectric layer and flip the ferroelectric domains, thereby reducing the device’s resistance. Conversely, under low-frequency pulses (10 Hz), the polarization level of the ferroelectric layer decreases, leading to an increase in device resistance. These phenomena are consistent with the paired-pulse facilitation and paired-pulse depression functions, the typical behaviours of short-term plasticity in biological synapses. The short-term and long-term coupled responses of second-order memristors exhibit substantial similarities to neuronal behaviour and are expected to be

# Three-terminal ferroelectric synapses

FeFETs, adopting ferroelectric materials as the gate dielectric layer, have received wide attention in the field of three-terminal ferroelectric neuromorphic devices. The electrical properties of FeFETs are closely related to the partial polarization switch in ferroelectric domains, where imposing a strong voltage can modulate the polarization of domains. The mechanism enabling multilevel conductive states in FeFETs (Fig. 3a) begins with applying a negative pulse to the gate to initialize polarization upward $( P _ { \mathrm { u p } } ) _ { \mathrm { \ell } }$ , causing electrons to be depleted in the channel, and results in low conductance. Then, applying a positive pulse, several domains are switched downward $( P _ { \mathrm { d o w n } } )$ and a few electrons accumulate in the channel, raising conductance and negatively shifting the $V _ { \mathrm { t h } } .$ . If positive pulses are continuously applied, the proportion of switched domains will increase until all domains are switched. Because of the non-volatile nature of ferroelectric polarization, the conductive state of the device persists even after the voltage is removed. According to this mechanism, the short-term plasticity and long-term plasticity can be realized.

FeFET analogue synaptic behaviour. In biological synapses, synaptic weight is determined by the number of receptors in the postsynaptic membrane. Long-term plasticity happens only when the number of receptors changes, whereas short-term plasticity is related to the number of neurotransmitters and receptor numbers remain unchanged. In a three-terminal ferroelectric synaptic device, the drain usually acts as the postsynaptic terminal and the gate serves as the presynaptic terminal. The channel conductance represents the synaptic weight and is reflected by the source-drain current $ { ( I _ { \mathrm { d s } } ) } _ { \mathrm { \Omega } }$ .

Short-term plasticity can be demonstrated in FeFETs by applying an electric single pulse (Fig. 3b), where the excitatory postsynaptic current (EPSC) or inhibitory postsynaptic current (IPSC) is modulated by the pulse’s polarity, amplitude and duration63,64. For n-type channels, positive pulses typically induce the EPSC and negative pulses result in the IPSC. When the pulse amplitude is moderate, EPSC peaks increase with stronger positive pulses and return to baseline quickly, whereas moderate negative pulses reduce the IPSC temporarily. Increasing pulse duration can achieve similar effects. However, if the pulse amplitude exceeds a threshold, the postsynaptic current (PSC) does not revert to its initial state $( I _ { 0 } )$ but stabilizes at a higher (EPSC) or lower (IPSC) level, transitioning short-term plasticity into long-term plasticity. This behaviour is attributed to the ferroelectric layer’s switching mechanism: domains with varying coercive fields switch polarization only when the applied voltage exceeds the minimum coercive field, causing non-volatile changes in channel conductance proportional to the number of switched domains.

Commonly, there are three pulse schemes to measure the LTP and LTD curve65 (see Supplementary Fig. 3). In pulse scheme 1, the synaptic weight is modified by identical pulses. In pulse scheme 2, the pulses maintain the same amplitude but increase in duration. Pulse scheme 3 raises the voltage with a fixed duration. Remarkably, the incremental voltage pulse scheme 3 provides the most exceptional synaptic performance with a high maximum conduction to minimum conduction ratio, and symmetric characteristics. In addition, this phenomenon was also reported in other studies of hafnium-based FeFETs63,66–68.

However, light modulation would aid in constructing neuromorphic vision systems because a certain polarization direction can be reversed under illumination (see Supplementary Information 6). These results demonstrate that ferroelectric synaptic devices can emulate both short-term and long-term plasticity through controlled polarization switching, with their EPSC or IPSC modulation and LTP or LTD curves being highly tunable via pulse schemes. However, the coexistence of ferroelectric switching with charge trapping and light-sensitive effects presents challenges for both reliable operation and opportunities for developing multifunctional neuromorphic systems, particularly for vision-processing applications.

Optimization of FeFET synaptic devices. To improve the performance of FeFET synaptic devices and address non-idealities such as limited dynamic range and asymmetric and non-linear synaptic weight updates, several methods have been developed.

In the FeFET synapse, the partial polarization of the ferroelectric layer modulates the channel conductance as the synaptic weight. However, in practical operation, it is the ON current that is utilized directly, not the conductance. The temperature leads to unexpected variation

of the ON current and an overlap between states, which decreases reliability. Enlarging the separation between the lowest $V _ { \mathrm { t h } }$ and the highest $V _ { \mathrm { t h } } ,$ , called the memory window, can help separate the overlapping states. The gate is the important control terminal, as the voltage applied to it affects the polarization. Thus, the split-gate structure is proposed to improve reliability (Fig. 4a). Here, the gate and underlying ferroelectric layer are separated into two parts by an insulator. The smaller ferroelectric layer area increases the highest $V _ { \mathrm { t h } } ,$ , and sensing from either of the gates lowers the lowest $V _ { \mathrm { t h } } .$ Thus, the memory window and the sensing margin are enlarged73,74

The interface layer also has a pivotal role in the performance and reliability of FeFETs (Fig. 4b). An HfO -based FeFET with an SiON interface layer between the ferroelectric layer and the channel shows excellent noise immunity and endurance compared with the $\mathrm { S i O } _ { 2 }$ interface layer75. This noise immunity could be due to the higher defect density in the SiON layer that contributes to the release of electrons trapped by interface traps. Moreover, inserting a TiN layer deposited by atomic layer deposition between the gate electrode (TaN) and the HZO ferroelectric layer results in symmetric and linear changes to the synaptic weight67. This could arise from the higher crystallinity of the ferroelectric orthorhombic phase, which contributes to remanent polarization $( P _ { \mathrm { r } } )$ , and the slower change between the $P _ { \mathrm { u p } }$ and $P _ { \mathrm { d o w n } }$ transition.

Improving the properties of the ferroelectric layer is another optimization method (Fig. 4c). The superlattice FeFET (Fig. 4c, panel i) is an approach for obtaining more-stable conductance states by using the gate stack of a superlattice of a ferroelectric–dielectric–superlattice ferroelectric76,77. In the superlattice ferroelectric, a depolarization field $( E _ { \mathrm { d e p } } )$ is created, owing to the incomplete screening of the polarization charges. To screen the $E _ { \mathrm { d e p } } ,$ the superlattice ferroelectric decomposes into a larger number of domains, broadening the coercive field distribution of the domains, and then stabilizes partial polarization states. Based on these two effects, designing a suitable ferroelectric–dielectric– superlattice ferroelectric stack can potentially access more intermediate polarization states. By employing this gate stack in HZO FeFETs, synaptic performance with a dynamic range of 80×, a near-ideal conductance profile $( \alpha _ { \mathrm { p } } { : } \alpha _ { \mathrm { d } } { = } { - 2 } { : - } 1 . 5 8 )$ and a large number of conductance states (1,000 states) are realized. Simulation calculations also confirm that the higher ferroelectric percentage decreases conductance variations and improves the linearity of FeFET synapses78. When scaling down an FeFET device, a thinner ferroelectric layer is required to decrease the $P _ { \mathrm { { r } } }$ and lower the operation voltage. However, when the ferroelectric layer is thinner than 5 nm, an undesired gate leakage current occurs and the property of the ferroelectric material degrades79,80. The nanocrystal-embedded insulator ferroelectric film scheme (Fig. 4, panel ii) is proposed as a solution81, in which a ferroelectric nanocrystal (ZrO ) is embedded in an amorphous ${ \bf A l } _ { 2 } { \bf O } _ { 3 }$ dielectric layer. Thus, the thickness

can be decreased to 3.6 nm, owing to the more stable ferroelectric phase in the nanocrystal-embedded insulator layer. In this device, the operating voltage can be lowered to 5 V. To decrease the area of a single FeFET device and improve the integration density, a nanosheet architecture can be utilized to construct an FeFET synapse device82. In a CMOS device, the nanosheet architecture has been proven to enable large current at a small area cost83 (Fig. 4c, panel iii). For an FeFET synapse, simulation research shows that an FeFET using a nanosheet architecture obtains a large dynamic range without a large area because its performance is improved by adding vertical tiers, not lateral area82

Although the non-volatility of ferroelectric polarization hinders short-term plasticity in ferroelectric synapses, it allows remarkable potential for long-term plasticity. As a result, improving the long-term plasticity of ferroelectric synapses has become a research priority84. The number of synaptic weights, as well as their linearity and symmetry in response to update pulses, profoundly affect information processing in neural networks. The linearity and symmetry can be improved by designing a suitable pulse scheme. The large dynamic range (>100) enables considerable differences between multiple weighted states, thus improving the mapping capability of synaptic weight. Training synaptic arrays requires frequent updating of the weights, which requires good endurance and retention of the synaptic devices37. Furthermore, energy consumption is a crucial factor in evaluating synaptic performance. Decreasing the operating voltage can achieve lower power consumption by reducing the thickness of the ferroelectric layer, which in turn can be achieved through device structures that optimize the quality of the ferroelectric layer. Optimizing the structure of the gate enhances the dynamic range73,74. An increase in the number of ferroelectric domains in the ferroelectric layer and the proportion of ferroelectric phase components allow the number of weight states and the device’s linearity to increase (see Supplementary Tables 2 and 3). Considering that analogue synaptic behaviours of two-terminal and three-terminal devices are both mainly controlled by ferroelectric polarization, some phenomena and optimization methods are also applicable to two-terminal devices.

# Ferroelectric-based neuron devices

An artificial neuron typically consists of three functional components: input spikes, an activation function and an output spike generator85. Various models have been proposed to describe the operation of neurons. In this section, we focus on the leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) and FitzHugh–Nagumo (FHN) models, emphasizing their circuit implementations, particularly for the LIF model.

# Leaky integrate-and-fire model

The LIF model is a simplistic yet widely used model in neural networks. In the LIF model, the neuron will integrate all of the pulses arriving at the end of the axon. Due to the leak effect, if a single input is too weak to reach the firing threshold, the membrane potential will slowly return to its resting state over time. This leakage is a result of the continuous exchange of ions across the cell membrane. During integration, when the membrane potential exceeds the threshold, the neuron emits a pulse. After the neuron sends a pulse, it enters a hyperpolarized state followed by a refractory period. This is described by one differential equation86,87:

$$
C \frac {\mathrm {d} V}{\mathrm {d} t} = - g _ {\mathrm {L}} (V (t) - E _ {\mathrm {L}}) + I (t) \tag {4}
$$

where C represents the membrane capacitance, $g _ { \mathrm { { L } } }$ is the conductance of the leaky resistor, V(t) represents the membrane potential, $E _ { \mathrm { L } }$ is the resting potential and I(t) is the input current from the synapse.

# Review article

The basic circuit model of the LIF neuron is shown in Fig. 5a. The capacitor is charged by I(t) and generates a potential V(t). If V(t) exceeds the threshold, the voltage-controlled switch will turn on and the capacitor will discharge to a resting potential $E _ { \mathrm { { L } } } .$ Meanwhile, a separate driver circuit will emit a spike. The reverse polarization characteristic of FeFETs is commonly utilized to achieve membrane potential reduction and pulsed output in the LIF model. The first FeFET-based spiking neuron involves one FeFET and one MOSFET as a switch to charge and discharge a capacitor (Fig. 5b). In this example, an N-metal-oxide semiconductor transistor and a capacitor are used to realize the leaky effect. However, the thick 100 nm $\mathrm { P b } ( Z \mathbf { r } _ { 0 . 2 } \mathrm { T i } _ { 0 . 8 } ) 0 _ { 3 }$ 3 ferroelectric exhibits a long retention time, which is not ideal for leaky neuron operation. Experimentally, a 5 nm partially crystallized HZO layer can enhance the $E _ { \mathrm { d e p } }$ and the leakage current, called the leaky ferroelectric layer, which reduce the retention time. The leaky property enables the FeFET to function as a leaky FeFET. By using a leaky FeFET, a capacitor-less neuron using only one transistor and one resistor has been proposed (see Supplementary Fig. 4a–c). This device could greatly reduce the hardware cost of the neuron88 (see Supplementary Information 7). In summary, leaky ferroelectric layers enable compact, retentiontunable FeFET neurons for efficient LIF model implementation in neuromorphic systems.

# FitzHugh–Nagumo model

The FHN neuron model has gained prominence for its ability to mimic the operation of biological neurons more accurately than the simpler LIF model. The FHN neuron is characterized by two differential equations89,90 (see Supplementary Information 8). Utilization of the hysteresis loop characteristic of the FeFET in the design allows for a simplified implementation of the FHN model. The circuit design involves using one FeFET, three MOSFETs and one capacitor86 (see Supplementary Fig. 4d and Supplementary Information 8). The structure of possible devices is very diverse and can be tailored to specific desired performances for applications (see Supplementary Table 4). Furthermore, two-terminal devices such as FTJs have been explored for their potential use in neurons (see Fig. 4e and Supplementary Information 9). These ferroelectric neuron designs highlight the flexibility and adaptability of ferroelectric devices.

Compatibility with circuit elements such as capacitors and resistors and standard CMOS fabrication processes is the basis for ferroelectric devices to realize a wide range of circuit designs that mimic neuron model topologies; for instance, FeFETs that are fabricated directly on an 8 nm silicon-doped HfO2-based ferroelectric layer with the gate stack of a 28 nm high-k metal gate91,92. In addition, compatibility with other 2D materials, such as graphene and MoTe , provides the possibility for ferroelectric neuromorphic devices to participate in higher-level tasks, such as in-sensor computing93. Building upon the implementation of individual neurons and synapses, the next logical step is to explore how these units can be organized into scalable arrays. Such array architectures are essential for constructing functional neuromorphic systems that can perform complex, large-scale computing tasks.

# Ferroelectric-based bio-inspired arrays

The human brain, distinct from traditional computing frameworks, has inspired extensive efforts to construct ferroelectric synapse-like arrays. These synaptic devices demand low programming energy, fast switching speed and good scalability. Resistive random-access memory has the advantages of good compatibility with the CMOS, simple structure and good scalability. However, it has a large conductance randomness

caused by the switching mechanism, which limits its learning accuracy in large-scale neural networks. Phase change memory is fast, accurate and durable, but the non-linearity, high reset power and large fluctuations in conductance affect its performance in synaptic devices. FeFETs, however, offer unique possibilities for ultra-dense, low-leakage and fast synaptic design.

Crossbar arrays are the predominant circuit approach to realize synaptic arrays. The basic 2 × 2 topologies form the foundational structure of crossbar array architectures94 (Fig. 5c) with a basic 1T-1FeFET cell (Fig. 5d). The channel conductance of the ferroelectric transistor is modulated by applying a programmable pulsed voltage to the gate, enabling either writing or erasing. The core operation in neural network computing, the multiply-accumulate operation, is executed by multiplying the cell’s voltage with the conductance, adhering to Ohm’s law and accumulating the resulting current in a column following Kirchhoff’s law. The complete process consists of cell-by-cell weight writing via programmed pulses, pixel-level multiplication calculations and column-level addition operations, and, finally, weight erasure via programmed pulses. Here, the FeFET assumes the pivotal role of storing and computing analogue weights. A 0T-1FeFET cell enables weighted summation and synaptic plasticity for neuromorphic computing91,95 and offers the highest ferroelectric synapse unit density in a crossbar array, but is susceptible to write disturbance, owing to the absence of a transistor.

In an 0T-1FeFET array, FeFETs in the same column share a single gate line, leading to undesired programming of devices in that column during write operation. Furthermore, write disturbance is a common issue in various memory arrays, including static random-access memory, spin transfer torque-magnetic random-access memory and resistive random-access memory. To mitigate write disturbance, an access transistor can be incorporated at the gate of the FeFET (Fig. 5d) that distinguishes between selected and unselected rows96 in a 1T-1FeFET cell. Thus, the programmed voltage pulse is only applied to the chosen row’s word line (WL) which connects to the access transistor’s gate, whereas a stable high voltage is applied to the chosen column’s source line (SL). Connecting two or more96 access transistors to the gate and drain of the FeFET (see Supplementary Fig. 5a, 2T-1FeFET) can also prevent the disturbance problem and increase read and write flexibility. Another problem of weight writing is the asymmetry and non-linearity. During weight updating, the I–V output characteristic of the 1T-1FeFET access transistor is non-linear even if the transistor is guaranteed to operate in the saturation region at both high pulse voltages and low pulse voltages.

Another architecture, the XT-FeFET (see Supplementary Fig. 5b), faces similar non-linearity challenges. The 2T-1FeFET presents a solution by combining two CMOS transistors, a pull-up pFET and a pull-down nFET, at the gate of the FeFET (see Supplementary Fig. 5a). While the weight updates, pulses from the pFET and nFET gates update the FeFET gate voltage and the channel conductance, keeping the pFET and nFET working in the saturation region. The 2T-1FeFET ensures that the charging and discharging current is independent of the gate voltage of the FeFET97, balancing the currents and the synaptic weight updates. In conclusion, the FeFET-based neurons contribute to the development of efficient, scalable synaptic arrays because of their advantages in the multiply-accumulate operation, including ultra-dense synaptic design, low leakage and fast switching speeds.

# Bio-inspired in-sensor computing

Fields requiring real-time processing and low power consumption, such as unmanned driving, have shifted the focus from bio-inspired computing-centric to data-centric architectures99. Data-centric architectures integrate sensing, memory and computing, thus reducing latency and energy consumption. However, challenges persist, including complex device structures and low photoresponsivity. Ferroelectric materials enable efficient in-memory sensing and computing, simulating functions of the human eye and brain for advanced pattern recognition and intelligent classification.

The human visual system is remarkably powerful, capable of instantaneously discerning an object’s location, size, shape, colour, direction of movement and speed, and can recognize these within a broad range of brightness levels100. Visual information processing can be broadly categorized into signal reception, preprocessing and advanced post-processing stages. The retina executes signal reception and preprocessing (Fig. 6a). The structure of the retina primarily comprises three layers: the photoreceptor layer, the bipolar cell layer and the ganglion cell layer. The photoreceptor cells enable clear vision across different brightness levels, known as light adaptation. Horizontal cells connect photoreceptor cells and bipolar cells, forming a centre-surround antagonistic structure by lateral inhibition around the receptive field and enhancing contrast. Ganglion cells have crucial roles in motion detection and edge detection, and transfer the preprocessed signals to the brain for advanced post-processing, such as image recognition and classification.

The array of photodetector devices emulates retinal preprocessing functions at different incident light wavelengths or different light intensities, which generate varying optical response along with distinct photocurrent relaxation times following the cessation of illumination. These are inherent characteristics of the detectors themselves (Fig. 6b, top left). However, the photoresponsivity and photocurrent relaxation time of retinomorphic devices constructed using ferroelectric materials are regulated by the state of ferroelectric polarization (Fig. 6b, top right). By selectively controlling the device’s polarization state, it is possible to amplify the target signal and suppress background noise,

thereby enhancing contrast and achieving selective attention akin to retinomorphic preprocessing capabilities (Fig. 6b, bottom; see Supplementary Information 12).

In these devices that implement image information preprocessing, there are often only positive weights. The lack of negative weights results in a substantial shortfall in advanced computing. In various image processing applications, the combination of positive and negative weights is essential for edge detection, feature extraction and classification, as well as machine learning, analogous to the ON and OFF types found in bipolar cells. Continuously adjusting positive and negative weights remains a challenge in bio-inspired computing. Traditional photodetectors have a fixed photoresponsivity, which is difficult to effectively control (Fig. 6c, top left); conversely, the photoresponsivity of detectors based on ferroelectric materials can be linearly controlled by electrical pulse stimulation. The detectors possess both positive and negative photoresponsivity (Fig. 6c, top right). According to this characteristic, an array for in-sensor computing with MAC operations is constructed (Fig. 6c, bottom), implementing high-level processing functions such as edge detection and pattern classification (see Supplementary Information 13).

Furthermore, biomimetic visual functions of ferroelectric materials have been emulated based on the non-volatility and reconfigurability of ferroelectric materials. Capabilities for information preprocessing include light adaptation, selective attention and contrast enhancement, as well as advanced computing functions such as image convolution, recognition and classification at the hardware level. The field of in-memory sensing and computing is interdisciplinary, involving materials, devices, circuits, algorithms and integration technologies. Considerable challenges remain, such as large-scale and high-quality material growth, subsequent device processing techniques, reconfiguring devices on a single chip for various types of information sensing and processing, and designing complementary circuits and algorithms. Additionally, the integration approaches for in-memory sensing and computing and the trade-offs between various performance metrics also warrant consideration.

# Outlook

Ferroelectric devices are a promising computing element for high-density and energy-efficient memory and computing applications, owing to their rich device physics, such as non-volatile polarization switching and multi-state controllability. Now, semiconductor electronics for ferroelectrics has resurged with the emergence of hafnium-based ferroelectrics, whose ferroelectricity is still maintained as the thickness is a few nanometres with CMOS compatibility.

Despite their advantages, several technological challenges still exist for ferroelectric devices in bio-inspired computing (Fig. 7). The HfO2-based devices have the wake-up effect, which is a special phenomenon observed in HfO -based films, arising from the redistribution of oxygen vacancies or electric field-induced phase transitions101. It can be depicted as the gradual increase in $P _ { \mathrm { { r } } }$ and coercive electric field $( E _ { \mathrm { c } } )$ in early cycles during the cycling measurement. To achieve wake-upfree ${ \mathsf { H f O } } _ { 2 }$ -based films and enhance stability, it is crucial to control the presence of oxygen vacancies. ${ \mathrm { H f O } } _ { 2 } .$ -based films in devices also suffer fatigue, which decreases the endurance and service life performance of the devices. Fatigue occurs in ferroelectric films when oxygen vacancies accumulate at grain boundaries and interfaces, resulting in a substantial decrease in $| P _ { \mathrm { { r } } }$ and an increase in leakage current. Furthermore, many factors affect fatigue performance, including the fabrication method and the elemental composition of the resulting film. Thus, optimizing

the interface quality and reducing the oxygen vacancies are important for relieving fatigue and reducing the wake-up effect.

At the device level, several problems persist. The first is scaling down the size of single ferroelectric devices. The target is to reduce the ferroelectric grain size to a few nanometres and maintain the ferroelectricity. Although multilevel domain switching is easily controllable for large-sized ferroelectric devices with multigrains and domains, nanoscale domains remain challenging, owing to their small number of randomly distributed structural grains, which leads to device-todevice and cycle-to-cycle variations, exacerbated by their sensitivity to the film growth process procedure, film thickness and doping concentration102. Therefore, analysing the crystallization mechanism and optimizing fabrication processes are simultaneously required for

grain engineering in ferroelectric materials. If the target of grain sizes of a few nanometres is achieved, this would enable hundreds of grains in the scaled ferroelectric devices, producing a stable and repeatable multilevel conductance as the non-uniformity in the micro-grain level averages out in the ferroelectric devices.

The second device-level challenge is the demand for a voltage scheme with identical pulses (including the pulse amplitude and width). The most commonly used scheme with progressive pulse amplitude or width causes more complex peripheral circuits and higher power consumption. This problem could be solved by controlling the speed of polarization switching, where a transistor-based current limiter is used to control the displacement current flow103. However, interface design engineering, such as metal–ferroelectric–metal–insulator–semiconductor structures, can help decrease the drop in the writing voltage and extend the endurance of devices105,106. Thus, more research is required at the device level to achieve miniaturization, high density, excellent uniformity and superior endurance in ferroelectric devices.

At the array level, brain-like in-memory computing requires high calculation accuracy, high energy and area efficiency. Scaling down ferroelectric devices limits the storage and computing performance of large-scale ferroelectric arrays, owing to increased instability. The fabrication of large-scale ferroelectric arrays with stabilizable programming capability will be the focus of future ferroelectric array research. Large-scale integration is not just in the 2D plane but is expected to make a breakthrough in three dimensions as well. Furthermore, ferroelectric arrays achieve multiple modes of in-memory computing: current-domain and time-domain computing in three-terminal FeFETs and charge-domain computing in two-terminal ferroelectric capacitors. In the future, a crucial point is to make use of a ferroelectric device’s flexibility and structural diversity to integrate multiple modules such as sensing, storage and computing into one ferro-CMOS chip to facilitate a high-performance sensory and computing system.

For applications, ferroelectric-based neuromorphic computing shows the potential for near-sensor and in-sensor computing. However, the manufacturing processes of sensory and computing units are incompatible because these two units rely on different materials and circuit designs. To implement integrated systems, new integration technologies should be explored, including 3D integration and 2.5D heterogeneous integration. To guarantee the sensitivity and accuracy of near-sensor and in-sensor systems, the architectures should be specifically optimized according to the application scenarios.

Notwithstanding the above technological challenges, ferroelectric devices are promising for bio-inspired computing with diverse

functionalities, being a key component in supporting workloads that are unattainable using CMOS-based memory technology.
