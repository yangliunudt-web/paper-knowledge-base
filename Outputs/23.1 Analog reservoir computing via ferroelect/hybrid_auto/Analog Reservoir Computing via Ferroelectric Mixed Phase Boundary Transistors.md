Supplementary Figure 11. Fitting parameters for volatile memory characteristics of volatile DG MPBTFTs. a Fitting parameters for various initial IDs. b Changes in ID according to various VPGMs. The relaxation process of the volatile DG MPBTFTs, observed after the removal of the PGM pulse, is fitted with the double exponential function as follows:

$$
I _ {D} = I _ {0} + A _ {1} e ^ {- t / t _ {1}} + A _ {2} e ^ {- t / t _ {2}}, \tag {1}
$$

where t denotes the time elapsed following the removal of the PGM pulse. The terms $t _ { 1 }$ and $t _ { 2 }$ represent two distinct characteristic time constants that are indicative of the rapid and gradual decay processes, respectively. The initial later stages of retention characteristics in ferroelectric devices are commonly attributed to the depolarization field and trapped charge, respectively [4–6]. The two time constants are highly correlated with the time interval between pulses when conducting 4- and 5-timeframe measurements. If the time interval is excessively longer than the time constants, the physical reservoir will lose all information from the previous input pulses

when the next input pulse is applied. Conversely, excessively short time intervals can significantly impair the system’s ability to process temporal data. Setting an inappropriate time interval between pulses relative to the time constants results in poorly separated final reservoir states with significant overlap, highlighting the importance of setting an appropriate time interval. The parameters A1, A2, and I0 are constants of the relaxation process. A1 and A2 modulate the amplitudes of the rapid and gradual decay components, and I0 represents the ID when the relaxation process is completed after sufficient time. By using the fitting parameters shown in the a and b, the relaxation process of the DG MPBTFTs can be accurately modeled, given the amplitude of the applied PGM pulse and the initial ID. c Experimentally measured ID of the DG MPBTFT and calculated ID using the model, when four PGM pulses with randomly assigned amplitudes are applied. The predictive capability of the model demonstrates a high degree of accuracy through the three sets of different PGM pulse trains.

Supplementary Figure 14. Device-to-device variations of volatile DG MPBTFTs. ID

evolutions of 10 volatile DG MPBTFTs in response to 16 different input pulse trains (4.0 V, $1 0 0 ~ { \mu \mathrm { s } } )$ exhibit device-to-device variations $( \sigma / \mu )$ of approximately 0.045 on average $( \sigma / \mu =$ 0.023 ~ 0.11). Although device-to-device variations are generally perceived as a challenge in semiconductor device reliability, they present a unique advantage in the context of reservoir computing. These variations inherently increase the diversity of the reservoir’s response to inputs, effectively expanding the reservoir size [7, 8]. This diversity enhances the ability of the ARC system to differentiate between similar input patterns, subsequently improving the system

performance. Therefore, device-to-device variations play a crucial role in optimizing the functionality of ARC systems, contributing positively to the robustness and efficiency of the system.

# Supplementary Note 1. Advantages of our approach in hardware-based ARC system

In the realm of hardware-based ARC systems, the fundamental challenge lies in material and device engineering; specifically, the necessity for devices that exhibit distinct volatile and nonvolatile switching characteristics to implement the reservoir and readout networks, respectively. There have been several studies using various devices to implement hardwarebased ARC systems. These ARC systems leverage the inherent physical properties of materials to emulate a dynamic reservoir and offer dual advantages. In addition to achieving comparable performance to software-based systems across a wide range of applications, such as pattern/speech recognition and chaotic system prediction [7, 9, 17–20], ARC systems also significantly enhance energy efficiency [7, 10]. Two-terminal memristors, the most widely used to demonstrate the ARC system, attracted attention owing to their high speed and scalability. Despite these advantages, the stochastic nature of the filament formation and rupture processes in memristors introduce large variability and compromise the reliability of the ARC system. These limitations can be overcome using alternative materials and devices that utilize ferroelectric polarization switching with a more deterministic switching mechanism [21]. Moreover, hafnium-based fluorite ferroelectrics exhibit fast switching speeds, low-power consumption [22–25], and high compatibility with conventional CMOS processes [26, 27]. However, most previously studied hardware-based ARC systems that utilize ferroelectric materials have been limited to the implementation of physical reservoirs [13, 16, 28, 29], and the full integration of the reservoir, readout network, and artificial neuron remains a significant challenge due to the requirement of distinct materials for each component. While Chen, Z. et al. used two-terminal ferroelectric diodes to implement both the physical reservoirs and readout networks [8], the inherent attributes of two-terminal diodes present substantial constraints in

the implementation of the neurons essential for the ARC system. The integration of neurons should be adequately explored for the full integration of the ARC components.

Furthermore, a persistent challenge in the field is a clear differentiation between reservoir states. Many studies have difficulty clearly distinguishing reservoir states [8, 12, 14– 16, 30–32]. Most of these studies demonstrated physical reservoirs capable of representing 16 reservoir states (4 bits). However, problems of state overlap and indistinct state differentiation often arise even when physical reservoirs represent only eight reservoir states (3 bits) [8]. Clearly distinguishable reservoir states are crucial for the effective processing and recognition of temporal data within ARC systems, directly influencing their performance and efficiency [12, 14–16, 33]. Various attempts have been made to mitigate these challenges, including strategies such as employing input encoding with various pulse intervals [12, 15]. Although these strategies enhance the distinction between reservoir states, their inefficiency in terms of area and power consumption detracts from the advantages of hardware-based ARC systems. Moreover, owing to the slow current decay characteristics of the physical reservoirs used in several studies, the time taken to acquire reservoir states has been a bottleneck, inadvertently impeding the processing speed of the overall ARC system [8, 15, 16, 28, 30, 31]. To address these challenges, we propose a material-device co-optimization method for the area- and energy-efficient ARC system utilizing HZO-based TFTs. Our ARC system has the following advantages:

1) We report, for the first time, MPBTFTs for ARC systems. MPBTFTs enable lowpower operation by having a high polarization magnitude at a relatively low voltage compared to anti-ferroelectric materials. Moreover, a large dynamic range and rapid current relaxation process facilitate a clear differentiation between reservoir states and ensure fast data processing capabilities. The material versatility of HZO facilitates the implementation of distinct volatile and nonvolatile memory characteristics. The polar o-phase of the HZO is used for nonvolatile FeTFT-based artificial synapse that requires multilevel conductance, and HZO near the MPB

is used as a physical reservoir and LIF neuron owing to its volatile memory characteristics. This study provides the possibility of neuromorphic applications leveraging the MPB, which was previously limited to use as a cell capacitor in DRAM technology.

2) In addition to material engineering, the DG configuration of MPBTFT further enhances the performance of ARC. The DG MPBTFTs with four terminals offer superior controllability and reliability compared to the commonly used two-terminal devices. A persistent challenge in the ARC is a clear differentiation between reservoir states, which many studies struggle with. Clearly distinguishable reservoir states without overlapping are crucial for the performance and efficiency of ARC. While various attempts, such as complex input encoding methods, have been made to mitigate these challenges, their inefficiency in terms of area and power consumption detracts from the advantages of ARC. By contrast, DG MPBTFTs enhance the reservoir state differentiation and successfully demonstrate expanded 5-bit reservoir states with minimal hardware burden, which has rarely been implemented in previous studies. Furthermore, DG MPBTFT-based LIF neuron processes both excitatory and inhibitory pulses within a single device, reducing system complexity and power consumption.

3) For the first time, the seamless integration of physical reservoir, readout network, and LIF neuron is achieved by leveraging volatile MPBTFTs and nonvolatile FeTFTs. The adoption of similar fabrication processes for both TFTs with HZO films enables their co-integration on a single wafer with high CMOS compatibility.

With this point of view, we believe that our MPBTFT-based ARC system opens a new paradigm for researchers in the field of hardware-based ARC and novel applications of MPBTFTs, and provides insights to address the limitations of conventional ARC systems.

# Supplementary Note 2. Volatile and nonvolatile HZO thin films for ARC systems

# (a) Implementation of volatile and nonvolatile HZO thin films

The characteristics of the volatile HZO-based TFTs for ARC systems are realized through HZO thin films near the MPB. In many previous studies, the origin of ferroelectric properties of HfO2-based ferroelectric materials is derived from the orthorhombic (o)-phase and tetragonal (t)-phase generated during the crystallization process of the $H _ { \mathrm { { f O } _ { 2 } - Z r O _ { 2 } } }$ mixed thin film [34–36]. The evolution of the phase in HZO thin film is determined by various conditions such as grain size, thin film thickness, and external stress. In this study, the characteristics of the HZO-based TFTs are optimized by modulating the composition ratio of $Z \mathrm { r } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ in the HZO thin film, which is the most dominant factor. The polarization switching dynamics of the HZO thin film vary depending on the $Z \mathrm { r } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ content.

In HZO thin films with high Hf composition, the monoclinic (m)-phase is formed abundantly. When the composition ratio of Hf and Zr are similar, o-phase formation is highly activated and exhibits ferroelectricity with hysteresis characteristics. By contrast, in HZO thin films with high Zr composition, the t-phase is formed dominantly and exhibits antiferroelectricity. The HZO thin films near the MPB that exist between these two crystalline phases have several metastable crystalline phases. Increasing Zr composition in the HZO thin film induces a phase transition from the o-phase to the t-phase owing to the larger asymmetric interstitial distance of $Z \mathrm { r } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ compared to HfO2, reducing the total energy of the grain and facilitating the phase transition. Consequently, higher $Z \mathrm { r } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ composition decreases coercive voltage (Vc) and eliminates the energy barrier between the two phases. The flattened mixed phase between the paraelectric and ferroelectric phases has no hysteresis and a high dielectric constant [37–41]. Owing to their high dielectric constant, HZO thin films near the MPB attract attention as thin films for dynamic random-access memory (DRAM), which requires high

capacitor performance [41–44]. Herein, we focused on the controllable hysteresis characteristics of the device.

It has been widely known that optimal ferroelectricity in HZO films is typically achieved with a ratio of $\mathrm { H f : } Z \mathrm { r } = 1 { : } 1$ . However, in our academic laboratory setting rather than an industry, we found that slightly adjusting the Hf ratio beyond the ratio of $\mathrm { { H f } ; \mathrm { { Z r } = 1 ; 1 } }$ led to enhanced ferroelectric properties. More stable ferroelectric properties are observed at a ratio of Hf:Zr ranging from 2:1 to 2.5:1 compared to the generally recognized optimal ratio of 1:1. Consequently, we opted for a ratio of $\mathrm { H f : } \mathrm { Z r } = 2 { : } 1$ for nonvolatile FeTFTs, which demonstrated optimal ferroelectric performance in our experiments. Given the critical role of ferroelectricity in the functionality of FeTFTs as synaptic devices, precise control over the composition of the HZO film is essential.

# (b) GIXRD analyses of volatile and nonvolatile HZO thin films

The distinctive electrical characteristics of the volatile DG MPBTFTs are attributed to the HZO thin films near the MPB. The deconvolution of the GIXRD spectra through the fixed peak positions reveals significant distinctions in the phase compositions of the volatile and nonvolatile HZO films (Supplementary Figures 4c and d). The non-ferroelectric phase (mphase) is minimally present in both the volatile and nonvolatile HZO films, highlighting their dominant ferroelectric properties. However, a significant difference exists in the ratio of the ferroelectric phases (o-/t-phases) between the two HZO films. In the nonvolatile HZO films, the ratio of the $\mathrm { o - / t - }$ phases is approximately 59%/34%, while the ratio is approximately 43%/53% in the volatile HZO films (Supplementary Figure 4e). This difference indicates a significant increase in the proportion of the t-phase within the volatile HZO films, attributable to the higher composition ratio of $Z \mathrm { r } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ . Conversely, the o-phase predominates in the nonvolatile HZO films.

# Supplementary Note 3. Advantages of IGZO FeTFTs with an MFMIS structure

The utilization of an IGZO channel material offers notable benefits, including the capability for fabrication at temperatures lower than those required to induce ferroelectricity in the HZO film [45]. This advantage is particularly significant as it allows for the integration of IGZO channels into ferroelectric devices without compromising the ferroelectric properties of the HZO film. Moreover, the FeTFTs with IGZO channel exhibit a high on/off current ratio, low-power consumption, and fast operation speeds [45–49].

The ferroelectric properties in the FeTFTs with an MFMIS structure can be enhanced by meticulously adjusting the area ratio (AR) between the metal–ferroelectric–metal (MFM) layer (AFE) and the metal–insulator–semiconductor (MIS) layer (AMOS) [49, 50]. This approach effectively modulates the capacitance ratio between the MFM and MIS layers within the MFMIS structure, dependent on the AR (AFE/AMOS) between the AFE and the AMOS. As the AR decreases while maintaining the AMOS, the capacitance of the MFM layer reduces. This reduction leads to an intensification of the electric field across the MFM layer, enhancing the polarization within the MFM layer and consequently improving the memory window (MV) of the FeTFTs. Moreover, precise capacitance matching between these layers significantly enhances the reliability of the FeTFTs by mitigating the electric field across the insulating layer [50–52]. The inner metal (IM) layer within the MFMIS structure plays a pivotal role in reducing cycle-to-cycle variation of the device by rectifying the inhomogeneous polarization in the ferroelectric layer [53]. The IM layer ensures a reliable device operation through a uniform electric field distribution and channel conductivity. Furthermore, the ferroelectric devices with MFMIS structure are renowned for their robust endurance characteristics [49, 54].

# Supplementary Note 4. Volatile DG MPBTFT-based physical reservoirs

Each volatile DG MPBTFT-based physical reservoir leverages the inherent memory effects of ferroelectric materials, characterized by their hysteresis behavior, to retain information regarding past inputs. This capability is indispensable for effectively processing time-dependent or sequential data because physical reservoirs respond to input signals over time rather than just responding to instantaneous input signals. In this study, the TG of DG MPBTFT is strategically utilized to improve physical reservoir characteristics, enhancing the performance and efficiency of the ARC system.

# (a) Volatile DG MPBTFT-based physical reservoirs without TG utilization

DG MPBTFT-based physical reservoirs can represent 16 unique binary combinations (4 bits) in total for binary inputs with 4 timeframes. As the number of applied input pulses increases, the number of reservoir states generated by the DG MPBTFT-based physical reservoirs increases exponentially. As the number of reservoir states increases, a corresponding increase in the overlap between states is inevitable. Therefore, addressing this issue is crucial for ensuring effective state differentiation and high system performance.

The nonlinearity and short-term memory characteristics ensure that the DG MPBTFTs exhibit distinctly separated final conductance states upon exposure to various input pulse trains (Figure 3h). Nonetheless, certain input pulse train configurations, such as the ‘1001’ and ‘0110’ inputs, may yield similar final conductance states. Physical reservoirs should generate distinguishable reservoir states for various input data to enable the processing and recognition of temporal data within an ARC system and achieve high performance [12, 14–16, 33]. Similar final conductance states that are not sufficiently differentiated significantly reduce the richness of the reservoir states, posing challenges in detecting and distinguishing subtle variations between input signals.

# (b) Volatile DG MPBTFT-based physical reservoirs with TG utilization

To address the challenges of generating distinguishable reservoir states for various input data within an ARC system, the TG of DG MPBTFT is utilized in this study to significantly enhance the differentiation of the final conductance states. Different input pulse trains were applied to the BG, while a fixed pulse scheme was applied to the TG (Figure 3i). The approach significantly enhances the distinctness between reservoir states, providing a clear differentiation. The rationale behind this improved distinctness lies in the impact of the bias condition applied to the TG on PGM efficiency. The application of gradually decreasing voltage pulses to the TG across the 4 timeframes augments the influence of the input data in the latter timeframe, resulting in a clear distinction between the final reservoir states. Furthermore, adequate utilization of TG enhances the ID change of the DG MPBTFT, which can achieve a broad spectrum of final conductance states. This characteristic suggests enhanced potential for more distinct separations between reservoir states.

The utilization of TG in DG MPBTFTs not only clearly distinguishes reservoir states but also enables the expansion of reservoir states. This capability marks a substantial advancement over previous studies that have rarely achieved a 5-bit representation of physical reservoirs. Previous studies that implemented 5 bits relied on input encoding methods utilizing varied pulse intervals, which considerably increased the area and power consumption [12, 15]. In contrast, DG MPBTFT-based physical reservoirs enable state expansion with a minimal hardware burden. Expanded reservoir states can enhance the area and energy efficiency of the MPBTFT-based ARC system. Note that by further optimizing the measurement conditions, a larger dynamic range and more distinct reservoir states can be obtained.

# (c) Measurement results of volatile DG MPBTFT-based physical reservoirs

responses according to various initial currents and VPGMs are shown in Figure 3f and Supplementary Figure 10, while the ID changes according to various pulse widths and VTGs are presented in Figure 3g and Supplementary Figure 12. When pulses of the same VPGM are applied, the ID changes decrease as the initial currents increase, and the ID is saturated. Additionally, the ID decays more rapidly as its magnitude increases. The ID changes increase with wider pulse widths, and decrease as the VTG increases when pulses are applied to the BG. These results are systematically organized in Supplementary Figure 11.

# Supplementary Note 5. Volatile DG MPBTFT-based LIF neurons

One notable advantage of the volatile DG MPBTFT-based LIF neuron is its capacity to process both excitatory and inhibitory pulses within a single device, enabling the versatile modulation of neuronal behavior. This unique capability is attributed to the opposing effects of the inhibitory pulses applied to the TG and excitatory pulses applied to the BG. While the excitatory pulses enhance the polarization of the HZO film toward the channel, facilitating an increase in ID, the inhibitory pulses revert this polarization, effectively reducing ID.

When inhibitory pulses with $V _ { \mathrm { i n h } }$ exceeding 3.0 V are applied after a sequence of excitatory pulse trains, the neuron does not fire with 13 excitatory pulses (Figure 4e). By contrast, when Vinh is less than 3.0 V, the neuron fires in response to the subsequent application of the same number of excitatory pulses. The inhibitory efficiency of LIF neurons increases as the $V _ { \mathrm { i n h } }$ increases.

When inhibitory pulses with widths (twidth,inh) up to 100 μs are applied, the neuron can fire in response to 13 excitatory pulses (Figure 4f). However, at an inhibitory pulse with twidth,inh of 500 μs, the neuron is fully inhibited and does not fire by the same number of excitatory pulses. The inhibitory efficiency of LIF neurons increases as the twidth,inh increases.

When one or two inhibitory pulses, each with a $V _ { \mathrm { i n h } }$ of 5.0 V and a twidth,inh of 100 μs, are applied, the neuron is able to fire after receiving 13 excitatory pulses (Figure 4g). On the contrary, the application of inhibitory pulses more than three fully inhibits the neuron, leading to a complete reset to its initial state. The inhibitory efficiency of LIF neurons increases as the number of applied inhibitory pulses increases.

# Supplementary Note 6. MPBTFT-based ARC system for handwritten digit recognition

The MPBTFT-based ARC system consists of a nonvolatile FeTFT-based readout network and volatile DG MPBTFT-based physical reservoirs and LIF neurons (Figure 5a). When the input pulses are applied to the physical reservoir, each DG MPBTFT responds dynamically. The state of the physical reservoir evolves over time in response to input pulses and is fed into the readout network. The readout network, which consists of a FeTFT array, generates outputs for the input pulses in the form of a current by combining the reservoir states based on their synaptic weights. When necessary, a pair of FeTFTs can be used to represent the positive and negative weights. The output currents are transmitted to the DG MPBTFT-based LIF neurons as excitatory or inhibitory pulses. This architecture enables efficient ARC, which is required for addressing complex systems, including images and temporal data. In this study, the simulations were performed using the measured electrical characteristics of the fabricated DG MPBTFTs and FeTFTs.
