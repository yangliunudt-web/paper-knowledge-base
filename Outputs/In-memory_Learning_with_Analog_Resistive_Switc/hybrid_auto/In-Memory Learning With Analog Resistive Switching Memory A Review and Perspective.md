# In-Memory Learning With Analog Resistive Switching Memory: A Review and Perspective

This article reviews the existing analog resistive switching memory devices and their hardware technologies for in-memory learning, as well as their challenges and prospects.

# I. I N T R O D U C T I O N

Ever since Hinton [1] published his phenomenal article in 2007, renewed interest in artificial neural networks (ANNs) has contributed significantly to the progress of

artificial intelligence (AI) and led to the explosive growth of deep learning. As one of the hottest research topics today, AI has generated widespread interest from both industry and academia, simultaneously causing remarkable social impact (e.g., Google’s AlphaGo [2]). Various tasks ranging from visual recognition, autonomous driving, voice assistance, chess playing, and many others have already been realized, and some AI applications have even achieved better cognitive accuracy than humans [3].

Hardware advancement is a key enabler in the rapid growth of AI applications. Hardware implementation of AI systems is typically done using central processing units (CPUs) or graphic processing units (GPUs). Also, custom-designed processors and accelerators have been used, for example, Manchester’s SpiNNaker [4], Zurich’s ROLLS [5], Heidelberger’s BrainScaleS [6], Google’s tensor processing unit (TPU) [7], and Tsinghua’s Tianjic [8]. In addition, several application-specific integrated circuits (ASICs) have also been developed based on silicon complementary metal–oxide–semiconductor (CMOS) technology (e.g., MIT’s Eyeriss [9], [10] and a variety of other accelerators [11], [12]). However, the ever-increasing demand for memory capacity and bandwidth makes it difficult to meet the growing need in the big data era. With the von Neumann architecture, the memory and the computing unit are physically separated. The growing disparity of speed between the processor and the memory storage is often referred to as the memory wall. Besides, the data need to be transferred back and forth between the memory and the computing unit. The limited data transfer bandwidth between CPU and memory leads to the von Neumann bottleneck and makes such architecture greatly constrained by computation delay and energy, thus becoming the most critical limitation. For this reason, computation-inmemory—where information can be processed and stored at the same locations—becomes one of the most desirable choices [13]. Because of the merging of data storage and computing units, the limitation of data transfer bandwidth and the speed mismatch between these two units are naturally resolved. Several prototype integrated circuits have been developed to realize computation-inmemory with traditional CMOS technology, for example, static random access memory (SRAM) [14], [15], dynamic random access memory (DRAM) [16], and flash memory [17], [18]. Significant improvements in speed and power consumption have thus been achieved. However, the limitations of these devices, namely high static energy consumption in SRAM and DRAM, low programming speed in flash memory, and large area of SRAM, limits further improvement in the energy, speed, and area efficiency. In addition, the difficulty of implementing in-memory learning in these traditional memories also restricts its application scenarios. The exploration of new devices with high speed, low power consumption, small area, nonvolatility, and in-memory learning capability has become an urgent priority.

With regard to overcoming the above-mentioned limitations, emerging nonvolatile memory (NVM) devices, such as resistive random access memory (RRAM) [19], phase change memory (PCM) [20], and ferroelectric devices (e.g., ferroelectric field effect transistor—FeFET [21] and ferroelectric tunneling junction—FTJ [22]) have gained significant interest. These emerging devices exhibit resistive switching based on different mechanisms to store information. Therefore, they can be grouped into a large category, namely resistive switching memory (RSM) devices. The crossbar structure of these device arrays makes it easy to realize the dot-product operation (see Fig. 1). The input voltage vector is applied to the corresponding device in one column (product), and the current generated by each device is naturally summed in a bitline as the output (sum). Compared with the conventional CMOS technology, RSM devices have many advantages, such as nonvolatile weight storage, fast parallel matrix multiplication, and ultrahigh integration density [23]–[27]. Another significant advantage is the potential for efficient in-memory learning, which is critical in the complex and dynamic environments for future “smart” systems and applications. However, it is worth noting that the relatively immature technology makes it difficult to be commercialized in the near future when compared with SRAM, DRAM, and flash memory. But we believe that the potential advantages of RSM devices, especially those with in-memory learning capabilities, could make them the workhorse to revolutionize AI hardware in the future. In the following subsections, we will conceptually introduce the RSM devices and the in-memory learning.

# A. RSM Devices

RSM devices can be classified into two categories— digital and analog—based on the electrical characteristics

of the devices when operated under a direct current sweep or a pulse signal. The mechanism and behavior of resistive switching in both filamentary and nonfilamentary devices have been widely investigated by the community [28]–[33]. Since it is not the focus of this

article, it is not discussed in detail here. Digital memory devices usually have an abrupt resistance switching feature during the programming operation. They exhibit an abrupt set and reset characteristic [see Fig. 2(a)] and show a similar sharp switching behavior when

being pulsed [see Fig. 2(b)]. After applying the initial pulse, the conductance stays constant as depicted in Fig. 2(b). Digital memory devices have been extensively investigated as potential alternatives to mainstream memories such as DRAM [34], [35] and flash memory [36], [37]. Some optimizations and designs of peripheral circuits also make it equally successful in embedded applications [38]–[40].

In addition, the advantages of high energy efficiency and high speed of these memory devices have led to the development of many designs of neural networks [41]–[43] and accelerators [44] in recent years. For example, implementations such as PRIME [45] and ISAAC [46] have demonstrated significant improvements compared to some ASIC solutions such as DaDianNao [47]. However, because of the digital nature, the existing realizations employ multiple devices to represent one synapse and require complicated peripheral circuits for shift and addition operations. Some published articles have envisioned the use of multilevel cell (MLC) devices to overcome the limitations of having multiple devices for one synaptic weight [48], [49]. Here, MLC devices often refer to devices with a small number of discrete conductance levels [see Fig. 2(b)]. A conductance window always exists between two adjacent levels without any overlapping distribution [50]. Although MLC offers the potential to enhance the learning ability of neural networks, the bitwidth, and asymmetry in the set and reset processes, and the complexity to use MLC devices still impose severe limitations.

In contrast to digital RSM devices (including the MLC device), analog RSM devices undergo a gradual resistance change, under either direct current sweep or pulse operation, as shown in Fig. 2(c) and (d). To obtain analog performance, different implementations have been investigated recently. These include the use of set-only two-RRAM per cell, reset-only two-RRAM per cell [51]–[53], multipleweak-filament RRAM [54], [55], and interfacial switching RRAM [23], [56]–[59]. The reason why two devices per cell can act as one analog switching device is that most of the existing devices are not completely digital. They usually have a one-side operation process that exhibits a gradual resistance change (e.g., gradual set for PCM and gradual reset for filamentary RRAM). When two devices are connected back-to-back in series, one device is always in the gradual resistance change state. One optimization technique for typical filamentary RRAMs is to use multipleweak-filament RRAMs. As its name implies, the conduction mechanism of such a device no longer depends on one pivotal conductive filament; instead, it makes use of multiple filaments to produce a gradual resistance change. This approach has the advantage that one can change the number of weak conductive filaments in order to adjust the amount of total resistance change. Different from filamentary RRAMs, an interfacial switching RRAM is naturally an analog switching device because of the mechanism of collective migration of oxygen vacancies at the interface.

# B. In-Memory Learning

As in-memory learning has generated considerable attention with regard to neuromorphic computing and neural networks, the in-memory learning capability of RSM devices has become an attractive feature. In comparison, off-memory learning is a relatively simple way to perform the learning of neural networks on the computer [60]. Each device is programmed to the target value so that the entire neural network has the correct function for inference. After writing, the synaptic weights are almost never changed. Therefore, off-memory learning is currently limited to tasks that do not need update the synaptic weights after programming. This is a serious drawback that limits its applications.

In-memory learning involves training the entire neural network on its hardware. The configuration of the entire network changes during learning with new incoming samples. This allows it to adapt to the complex and varying environments and mitigate the degradation of recognition accuracy caused by device variations compared with offmemory learning. In detail, the specific solution obtained by off-memory is not necessarily suitable for all chips, and it is not realistic to train each chip on the servers. However, in-memory learning could obtain many solutions and realize in situ weight update; hence, it is possible to adapt to the variation among different chips. In addition, the concern of computing is whether the sum of currents from the bitline meets the expectation. The in-memory learning could mitigate the effects of stuck devices by appropriately changing the conductance of surrounding devices, thereby obtaining the results similar to the expected values. These unique advantages make in-memory learning an attractive option for neuromorphic computing and neural networks.

RSM devices are particularly well suited for this purpose, which is the key reason for its growing popularity [61]. Compared with digital memory devices, analog RSM devices are more suitable for realizing in-memory learning. They have more intermediate states contributing to more accurate computing, require less area, and consume less power than digital approaches. Although these devices offer considerable potential, they still face several key challenges such as nonlinearity, asymmetry, small number of levels, and large variation, which will need to be addressed before they can be used for largescale implementations.

Some characteristics of resistive devices can also be alleviated with in-memory learning. For example, variations are inevitable in RSM devices, regardless of whether they are digital or analog RSM devices. The variations have a serious impact on the result of the summation of the dot-product in array implementations. For example, consider one column of an array with ten devices and assume that the conductance of each device is 3% above the standard value. The actual output current will be much higher than the expected result, thereby resulting in potential loss of recognition accuracy. In-memory learning

can alleviate this problem through its overall configuration of the network during the learning process. The synergistic adjustment of all devices makes it relatively tolerant to device variations, and therefore the adverse effects caused by device nonuniformity can be largely avoided. For this reason, in-memory learning with RSM devices is a good choice to realize neural networks and neuromorphic computing.

In recent years, there have been many representative review articles that discussed the computation-in-memory from different perspectives, including device model and characterizations [31], resistive switching materials and mechanisms [62], computing schemes [63], reliability [64], bio-inspired characteristics [65], and hardware implementations [66], [67]. However, a comprehensive review article, which provides a top-down view to understand the specific requirements on the device characteristics, is still highly demanded. Therefore, in this review, we focus on analog RSM devices and the requirements for in-memory learning. First of all, to make readers have a large picture of the existing hardware implementation of in-memory learning, the high-level design considerations, from circuit to architecture and algorithm, are reviewed and analyzed in Section II. Then, the definitions of figures of merit (FoMs) for analog RSM devices are provided in Section III. Subsequently, a detailed end-to-end analysis based on the high-level considerations is conducted to understand how these FoMs affect the performance of in-memory learning system, and a quantitative guideline table for device requirements is summarized. Furthermore, the state-of-the-art optimization methodologies in order to meet those FoM requirements are reviewed in Section IV. Finally, this article is concluded with a discussion of the current challenges and future prospects of analog RSM devices, which will serve as a useful roadmap for future research and development.

# II. C R O S S - L A Y E R C O N S I D E R AT I O N F O R I N - M E M O R Y L E A R N I N G S Y S T E M

Different from the conventional IC design technology, the development of in-memory learning system always calls for cross-layer codesign from device level to system and algorithm levels [68], [69]. FoMs of analog RSM device actually have strong correlation with system architecture and algorithm. A well-designed in-memory learning system can significantly relax the device requirements. Therefore, a cross-layer overview can help understand the practical approaches for both system implementation and device optimization. Considering the fact that circuit, architecture, compiler, and algorithm are closely related to one another, it is difficult to make an independent overview of each level separately. Instead, we divide into three sections: network mapping (Section II-A), inference with multilayer network (Section II-B), and in-memory learning (Section II-C) to comprehensively analyze the design considerations from circuit to algorithm.

# A. Network Mapping

As the basis for inference and learning, mapping is a very important part for hardware implementation of neural networks. Different from traditional digital memory, an analog RSM device uses its actual conductance value to represent the weight of neural networks rather than fix values after quantization (e.g., 3 bit, “0–7”). How to represent floating point number with a limited conductance range must be considered carefully. Many methods have been developed for different types of neural networks, including multilayer perceptron (MLP) [27], [70]–[72], convolutional neural networks (CNNs) [73]–[75], spiking neural networks (SNNs) [76]–[78], Hopfield networks [79], restricted Boltzmann machines (RBMs) [80], and so on. The representative mapping strategies are reviewed below, including two parts: the mapping from an input value to electrical signals (the input coding pattern) and the mapping from positive or negative weights to device conductance (full weight representation).

1) Input Coding Pattern: The input coding pattern usually refers to the process of converting the information required by the cognition task into a voltage operation scheme. For the classical MNIST application, it is the grayscale information of each pixel (0–255). The amplitude-dependent analog inputs [81]–[87] and timedependent digital inputs [70], [85] are the two most common choices.

The coding pattern of amplitude-dependent analog inputs is displayed in Fig. 3(a). The voltage amplitude is divided into 256 levels to meet the input information requirements. Such a pattern could perform dot-product operations with high energy efficiency, but there are still some issues that need to be considered.

The coding pattern of time-dependent digital inputs is shown in Fig. 3(b) and (c). The number of pulses [see Fig. 3(b)] or the width of the pulse [see Fig. 3(c)] is encoded as the input information. Intuitively, it is inferior in the efficiency of the dot-product operation compared to the amplitude-dependent analog inputs (at least 256 times in the worst case). However, one of the advantages is that it is less affected by nonideal factors like I–V nonlinearity, which makes it appealing to achieve better computation

accuracy. To reduce the extra time overhead, a binary encoding method based on shift registers has been developed [69], [88], which could reduce the maximum number of input pulses from 256 to 8. The binarization of input information on the algorithm [88] is acceptable; however, the tradeoff with accuracy loss is inevitable.

2) Full Weight Representation: The positive and negative weights enhance and suppress the input information, respectively, which is the basis for the neural networks to achieve correct classification. The analog RSM device always has a positive conductance; hence, how to realize full weight is a key issue that must be solved. Differential pair structure [81], [83], [85], [89], [90] and peripheral circuit processing [82], [88] are two main solutions. The differential pair uses two analog RSM devices to represent one synapse. The currents generated by the positive and negative devices are subtracted at the adder to obtain the output of this layer [see Fig. 4(a)]. Such a realization doubles the number of devices; however, considering the highdensity integration of analog RSM devices, it is usually affordable. The approach of peripheral circuit processing is

more complicated. Liu et al. [82] proposed an approach to realize full weight with one device by appropriately setting the reference voltage in the current-to-voltage converter [as in Fig. 4(b)], while maintaining the isolation between the weight arrays and peripheral circuits. Chen et al. [68], [88] developed a comprehensive simulation framework and system, in which the cooperation of the input vector and the shift register made the full weight realization with one device possible [see Fig. 4(c)]. In such a design, the input vector first performs the dot-product operation. Then, the obtained result is doubled by the shift register and a new output is obtained. Finally, the new output result subtracts the result of the dot-product (realized by the accumulator) between the input vector and an all-one matrix. These considerations are very general for most of the application demos. For more complex neural networks, it may be necessary to find new mapping methods to adapt to the crossbar structure. For example, Gao et al. [73] extended the use of RSM devices to CNNs. They employed a $1 2 \times 1 2 \mathrm { H f O } _ { x }$ crossbar array to demonstrate the feasibility of unfolding a 2-D kernel matrix into a 1-D column vector to implement a convolution kernel. Gokmen et al. [74] mapped convolutional layers to fully connected resistive processing unit (RPU) arrays, allowing one to fully utilize the parallel structure of the hardware for the backpropagation (BP) algorithm. Each column of the device array represents a convolutional kernel, which means several 1-D column vector convolutional kernels that are placed side by side and can be operated simultaneously. Then, the outputs corresponding to the receptive field input of each of the convolutional kernels appear in parallel. These initial explorations provide important clues for the implementation of CNNs and even more complex neural networks using RSM devices.

# B. Inference With Multilayer Network

After effective mapping, we then discuss how to implement inference and learning in Sections II-B and II-C. Since the learning process also includes inference stage and weight update stage, in this part, we first discuss the design considerations of inference. The main focus of inference process is the data transfer from array to array, including the conversion of current output into voltage signal and the quantization of analog output signals.

Because of the similarity of functions to biological neurons, the process unit of information transfer between different layers in ANNs is usually called artificial neurons. The artificial neuron functions are originally accomplished by computer software, including data transfer between layers, activation function, and information processing [72], [85], [91]. Then comes a series of circuit designs, including both analog and digital circuits. These circuit designs are usually composed of three parts, namely current conversion circuits, activation function circuits, and output circuits. The current conversion circuit mainly includes an analog-to-digital converter (ADC) [68], [88], integrateand-fire (IF) circuits [81], [92], and a sense amplifier (SA) [84]. The activation function circuits are usually realized by digital circuits. Then the output circuit (the digitalto-analog converter—DAC [81], [82], [84], [85] or 1-bit switch [88], [90]) converts the information processed by the activation function into a voltage pulse as the input of the next neuron layer, which determines the precision of data transfer between layers.

Although the existing circuit design can realize functions similar to artificial neurons, the use of DACs and ADCs can greatly reduce the advantages of analog RSM devices

in terms of area, power consumption, and integration density. Xue et al. [93] designed a more efficient MLC SA to improve the system efficiency. Wang et al. [94] made efforts to alleviate these overheads by reducing the precision of the ADC; however, an unexpected accuracy loss can occur easily if the number of bits of the ADC is reduced [69]. Considering that the requirements of different layers of the neural network are usually not exactly the same, a hybrid design with different precision ADCs have the potential to balance the tradeoff between overhead and accuracy. The data transfer in the analog domain is expected to further improve this situation. However, many innovations are required in the future research, involving algorithms, compensation circuits, architectures, and so on. Also, simple and efficient artificial neuron designs will be needed.

To simplify the concept, these various implementations can be abstracted into two categories: full or multibit precision and most significant bit (MSB) or sign. Full or multibit precision requires a complex circuit design and a large amount of register overhead for the hardware implementation of the learning algorithms. While the existing MSB or sign implementation has proved its feasibility with hardware efficiency, there is a certain compromise in the recognition accuracy.

# C. In-Memory Learning

Compared with mapping and inference, the design considerations for learning are more complex. For in-memory learning, there are two aspects need to be considered: how to efficiently calculate weight update value, and how to efficiently tune the conductance in the presence of device nonideal effects. Different from software training, in-memory learning usually avoids the complex calculations of weight values to improve the area and energy efficiency. In this case, the learning algorithms should be modified to adapt the hardware implementation with analog RSM devices. Here, we first briefly introduce several learning algorithms, and then, two milestone works as a case study are comprehensively reviewed. Several recent advances in algorithm optimization for hardware learning are summarized in the end.

Several classical algorithms have been widely investigated with the expectation to be compatible with RSM device arrays, including Manhattan update rule [95], delta rule [96], BP [97], and spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP). The Manhattan update rule only provides an update direction by comparison with the expected output values [70], so it can be achieved by a set or reset pulse when the device conductance needs to be updated. The delta rule gives further weight adjustment values, which are realized by adjusting the duration and amplitude of the operational pulse [70], [98]. STDP is usually realized by sending a frequency-dependent pulse signal in the preand postneurons [99].

In order to provide a more in-depth understanding of how these concepts (combining the network mapping

and in-memory learning) are applied in actual functional demonstrations, two milestone works are reviewed here in detail. The first hardware implementation of perceptron algorithm based on a pure RRAM array was reported by Prezioso et al. [27]. They built a 10 × 3 perceptron network using 60 $\mathsf { A l } _ { 2 } \mathsf { O } _ { 3 } / \mathrm { T i O } _ { x }$ interfacial switching RRAM devices. As shown in Fig. 5(a), the overall network has 10 input neurons, 30 synapses, and 3 output neurons. A differential pair structure has been employed, where one device represents a positive weight and the other device represents a negative weight. It is a crossbar structure consisting of pure analog RRAMs. Although the array size is small, it still achieves good inference accuracy for a specific input coding pattern of binary pixels [see Fig. 5(b)]. The effects of different initial resistance states have also been investigated. When using a Manhattan update rule for the learning process in which the neuron characteristics were simulated, a similar final conductance distribution for 60 devices was obtained. However, in order to reach a nearly perfect accuracy (zero misclassified pattern), the learning epochs show considerable discrepancy as shown in Fig. 5(c). This demo shows the feasibility of hardware implementation of perceptron network using analog RSM devices and reflects its potential to construct more complex neural networks.

Later on, a larger-scale array (128 × 8) implementation of perceptron network was demonstrated by Yao et al. [70] based on one-transistor–one-resistor

(1T1R) analog RRAM. By using a serial transistor within each RRAM cell, the issue of sneak path and the difficulty in realizing current compliance were both resolved, and therefore a relatively large array could be realized as compared with Prezioso’s work described above. A typical bidirectional gradual resistance switching was achieved in a $\mathrm { T a O } _ { x } / \mathrm { H f A l O } _ { y }$ RRAM stack. The mapping of one-layer neural network including the input, output, and weights to the 1T1R array composes the whole perceptron system [see Fig. 6(a)], which is implemented using a fully CMOS-compatible fabrication process [see Fig. 6(b)]. By selecting the number of input pulses according to the input value, the network is adjusted to recognize and classify grayscale images of faces from the Yale Face Database [98]. Additional face samples and constructed images with noisy pixels were also tested. Fig. 6(c) shows the learning process flow with two kinds of update rules—Manhattan update rule and delta update rule with write-verify, where average recognition rates of 85.04% and 88.08%, respectively, were obtained on noisy test patterns. Comparing the scheme with write-verify and the scheme without writeverify, each has pros and cons: the former provides 4.61× faster converging speed, 1.05× higher recognition accuracy, and 4.41× lower energy consumption compared to the latter, whereas the latter simplifies the write operation, to a large extent, compared to the former. The success in this application of Yale Face Database classification confirms the feasibility of hardware implementation of simple neural networks with large-scale 1T1R RRAM arrays.

These studies have made valuable early explorations for the hardware implementation of in-memory learning. However, they only implement simple single-layer perceptron. In the current stage, there are still many difficulties in the hardware implementation of multilayer in-memory learning, such as the BP of computing errors and the storage of intermediate computing results. The nonideal weight update and potential catastrophic forgetting could also be problematic. In addition, although successful functional demonstration using RRAM arrays confirms the potential of using analog RSM devices for neuromorphic computing, the recognition accuracy achieved in hardware implementation so far (e.g., MNIST, <98%) is still lower than the value achieved in software or digital ASIC. These issues need to be given enough attention and are expected to be addressed through further device optimization and codesign of architecture and algorithm in the near future.

In order to make existing software algorithms applicable to hardware, several algorithm optimizations for the inmemory learning process have been developed by the community. For example, Zhang et al. [100] proposed a sign BP (SBP) algorithm to realize in-memory learning. With binary interfaces (0, 1) in the forward process and 2-bit (±1, 0) in the backward process, the SBP can achieve a comparable classification accuracy with MLP on the MNIST data set, while reducing the area and energy cost without calculating and storing the intermediate results. Negrov et al. [101] described an approximation to the

BP algorithm. A series of pulses that trigger multiple positive or negative updates of the synaptic weight is used to represent the values of both the input signal and the backpropagated delta value, and a minimized operation is used instead of the product of these two values. The simulation results show that such approximation to the BP algorithm has good convergence and is expected to be used in the implementation of multilayer neural networks based on analog RSM devices. Lim et al. [102] proposed an adaptive learning rule to realize a full hardware neural network, which is capable of tolerating the nonlinearity and variations in the devices to some extent. Krestinskaya et al. [103] designed analog circuits to realize the BP algorithm. Yao et al. [75] developed a hybrid training method that combined on-chip and off-chip learning to accommodate device imperfections and improve the overall system performance. These advances have greatly promoted the implementation of in-memory learning on hardware. However, many designs do not consider the nonideality of the devices, while the circuit implementations are still inefficient. For these reasons, further algorithmic optimizations are needed to compensate for the circuit and device nonidealities.

# III. C O M P R E H E N S I V E A N A L Y S I S O F R S M C H A R A C T E R I S T I C S F O R I N-M E M O R Y L E A R N I N G

As we have discussed in the previous sections, the hardware implementation of in-memory learning is a crosslayer task with undividable interaction from device and circuit to architecture and algorithm. Among these layers, the device layer as the basis of such systems can directly impact the design of higher layers. It has become a critical and important issue as to: 1) what device characteristics significantly affect the performance of system; 2) what kind of target characteristics should be achieved to make the system work properly and avoid excessive overhead that overwhelms the advantage of in-memory learning. Therefore, the factors of analog memory devices that affect the in-memory learning system should be

carefully discussed. Recent work has led to some generally accepted conclusions about the key device characteristics, for example, linearity, symmetry, and level numbers as well as variation and fluctuation [67], [88], [91], [104]. However, exploring a specific FoM or device may lead to a lack of generality, which makes it difficult to come up with an accurate guideline for device requirements. In addition, a detailed analysis of the causes for the limitations of each FoM has been lacking so far. A thorough and in-depth discussion is hence crucial and necessary.

In this section, the key device characteristics and FoMs of analog RSM devices are first introduced (Section III-A). Then an in-depth analysis of how RSM device characteristics influence in-memory learning is conducted in Section III-B. In addition, a two-level classification is applied to make the relevant discussions more systematic and hierarchical.

# A. Key Device Characteristics and FoMs

Considering the evolution of analog switching devices, some of the FoMs are the same as those in a typical digital memory device (e.g., size, yield, speed, retention, energy consumption, and compatibility with post-CMOS processing). However, there are several aspects that are unique for analog RSM devices such as linearity, symmetry, dynamic range, level numbers, and endurance. For convenience, we choose an RRAM as a representative device to illustrate these concepts.

The linearity and symmetry of analog RSM devices are considered as the two most important requirements for in-memory learning applications. Linearity describes the relationship between the ordinal number of intermediate states and the corresponding conductance change value (-G). Symmetry refers to the similarity of such relationship between the set and reset processes. A dynamic range is the ratio between the maximum conductance and minimum conductance, which is typically in the range from 2× to 100× [67]. The level numbers indicate the number of intermediate states available within the dynamic range.

Fluctuations and variability (also known as a nonuniformity issue) are inevitable nonideal effects in analog RSM devices caused by the resistive switching mechanism. Typically, it involves two aspects, namely cycle-to-cycle and device-to-device variations.

Unlike that in traditional digital switching memory, the definition of endurance is more controversial in analog RSM devices because of the numerous intermediate states. In digital memory devices, endurance usually refers to the maximum number of high and low resistance state transitions (one set and one reset represent one cycle) that can be made while maintaining proper device operation, which is typically around $1 0 ^ { 6 }$ [105]–[107] and may even extends up to $1 0 ^ { 1 2 }$ [108]. In analog RSM devices, conductance switching mainly occurs between intermediate states, while digital-like full-range switching appears only in a few specific cases. Retention assesses the device conductance drift over time, corresponding to the capability of keeping the data. As a key parameter for device reliability, retention has been extensively studied in various RSM devices, and its dependence on resistance switching mechanism has been widely recognized.

Yield refers to the proportion of devices that work properly, which directly impacts the cost. With improvements in technology and the amelioration of the device architecture via introducing a serial transistor or selector, a high yield exceeding 99% has been obtained for digital memory application (e.g., 100 $\mathrm { H f O } _ { x } / { \mathrm { S i O } } _ { x }$ -based devices with various areas [109] and 16-Mb $\mathrm { T a O } _ { x } / \mathrm { H f O } _ { 2 }$ -based devices with 130-nm CMOS process [41]). However, the actual yield numbers of analog RSM devices are not available in the relevant articles, which is likely attributed to the difficulty in finding a universal guideline to distinguish device variation and yield. In this article, a digital-like criterion to check whether the resistive switching properties exist or not is used to define the analog switching device yield. The failure mode mainly consists of stuck-at-1 and stuck-at- $\cdot 0 ,$ corresponding to the high-resistance state (HRS) and the low-resistance state (LRS), respectively. Stuck-at-0 occurs in the forming process of the original device with an HRS,

which is reflected as a failure of forming. As for stuck-at-1, it could result from many situations, such as short circuit of a device in the initial state and dielectric breakdown during the programming process. Stuck-at-1 is usually more common and is more harmful to the overall neural network or system because of larger leakage currents. It is noteworthy that sometimes employing a strong pulse can make a “failed” device that has run thousands of cycles to go back to normal.

Area refers to the size of a single analog RSM unit in the device arrays (usually evaluated by the feature size F). Speed refers to the operational time required for one-time data rewrite or weight adjustment. Energy consumption includes both dynamic and static energy consumptions, and the former usually dominates in various applications. Other FoMs such as operational voltage and compatibility

with post-CMOS processing are also important to evaluate an analog RSM device. For example, the supply voltage affects the device reliability and energy consumption to a large extent, while CMOS-compatible manufacturing methods are advantageous because there is no need to develop new microelectronics manufacturing and processing technologies.

A succinct definition as well as the relationships between some device characteristics and synaptic weights of the neural network are summarized in Table 3 [78], [88], [90].

In addition, in order to give the reader a more intuitive understanding of the current analog RSM devices, a review of representative analog RRAMs and their key metrics are given in Table 4. Nonfilamentary and interfacial devices usually require a large operational voltage

and long pulsewidth compared to filamentary devices, which has been regarded as a major issue that limits their application so far [27], [57], [59]. But the current of its LRS is much lower than filamentary devices and hence it is theoretically possible to support a parallel computing array with a larger array size. In comparison, benefiting from the filamentary switching mechanism [33], [110], [111], the filamentary devices generally have better retention.

# B. Analysis of FoMs’ Impact on Neural Networks

In order to analyze the impact of these FoMs on neural networks, a device-to-application simulation system is developed. The simulation system consists of a two-layer perceptron neural network. A representative application— MNIST handwriting recognition—is adopted as a quantitative reference. Following the considerations summarized in Table 2, the neural network uses: 1) a differential pair structure to realize the full weights; 2) binary data of crosslayer in inference to simplify the data transmission; and 3) amplitude-dependent analog signals to represent the input coding pattern. Considering the characteristics of the MNIST data set (ten classes, 28 × 28 pixels, gray image), a $7 8 4 \times 2 0 0 \times 1 0$ network is used to obtain as much input signal information as possible and to retain the pivotal cross-layer data features. In addition, data normalization and standardization are applied to eliminate the impact of dimensions as well as to accelerate convergence. Based on this neural network, a series of simulations were performed to obtain an insight on the relationship between recognition accuracy and the key device parameters for in-memory learning. The relative convergence efficiency (RCE) [the ratio of the number of iterations required to achieve stable recognition accuracy (N) to the optimal result in the evaluated FoMs; see formula (3.1.1)] is applied to quantify the convergence speed of different conditions

The FoMs of analog RSM devices can be divided into two categories: Level 1 and Level 2. Level 1 parameters represent the FoMs that are essential for achieving sufficient functionality of a system, which include linearity, symmetry, dynamic range, level numbers, fluctuation, variability, yield, and failure mode. It should be mentioned that some FoMs, such as linearity and symmetry, which are very important for in-memory learning, may not be critical for off-memory learning when techniques like “chip-in-theloop” training can be used to address the device nonideal characteristics from the algorithm level [113], [114]. Level 2 parameters refer to the FoMs that make a functional system more efficient and reliable, which include area, operational voltage, energy consumption, speed, endurance, retention, and compatibility with post-CMOS processing.

It is worth noting that a “reference device” used in the simulation is based on the representative analog RSM devices listed in Table 4. This is chosen for its universality in terms of further device optimization. Its main FoMs are as follows: a nonlinearity of $" 2 "$ in the set process and a nonlinearity of $^ { 6 6 } - 2 ^ { 9 9 }$ in the reset process (which also imply symmetry), “10” for dynamic range, “128” for level numbers, and so on. Unless otherwise stated, all the simulations discussed later are based on this reference device. The following sections are organized according to this two-level classification. It should also be mentioned that the results of accuracy loss are obtained by comparing it to the highest accuracy from numerical on that can be obtained in such a neural network.

# 1) Level 1 FoMs:

a) Symmetry and linearity: The symmetry of the I–V switching curve can usually be divided into two categories, which are mainly determined by the level numbers and linearity. The former refers to the difference in available intermediate states between $G _ { \mathrm { m i n } }$ and $G _ { \mathrm { m a x } }$ in the set and reset processes. The latter refers to the different degrees of linearity in the set and reset processes. Fig. 7(a) shows the simulation results for the case of 128 intermediate states in the set process while gradually increasing the level numbers in the reset process. The different curves correspond to different linearity measures. The accuracy loss first declines and then increases slowly. There exist a specific number of intermediate states in the reset process that leads to the lowest accuracy loss. Furthermore, as the nonlinearity increases, asymmetry seems to have a positive effect on the accuracy. However, this is actually a compensation coming from the level numbers to the adverse effect introduced by the nonlinearity. When the linearity is at a high level, the improvement of level numbers has little effect on accuracy, and the symmetry becomes the dominant factor in this case. Thus, any asymmetry will lead to an accuracy loss. When the linearity is at a low level, the level numbers will be dominating, and therefore an increase in one-sided-level numbers can eventually reduce the harmful influence of nonlinearity. In this case, the symmetry due to level numbers becomes a secondary factor whose impact is insignificant until it decreases and becomes the dominant factor. As for the symmetry due to linearity, a quantitative assessment is conducted because of the prevalence of such phenomena in existing devices. Increasing the absolute nonlinearity in either the set or reset process results in larger accuracy loss as shown in Fig. 7(b). A symmetry of less than 1 is expected within the range of the evaluated nonlinearity to avoid an accuracy loss of more than 1% compared to ideal devices.

Essentially, whether the asymmetry results from level numbers or linearity depends on the differences in synaptic weight change (-G) during learning in the set and reset directions. Once the amount of conductance change in one direction is much larger than that in the other direction, it is difficult for the neural network to converge

to a relatively optimal weight distribution. This is because such asymmetry causes a very large weight adjustment in the opposite direction after several slow unidirectional optimization steps to approach the optimal solution. In addition, the difference in operational pulses (both in amplitude and width) in the set and reset processes is another common manifestation of asymmetry. When such highly asymmetric devices are deployed in actual systems, there will be higher requirements with regard to the design of the peripheral control circuits. It will add a significant overhead in terms of area, circuit complexity, cost, and so on. From these perspectives, a good symmetry is desirable for analog RSM devices used in inmemory learning applications. The simulations performed by Yu [67] and Chen et al. [88] confirm our conclusions about symmetry. Considering the different degrees of symmetry in different devices and the nonlinear curve shape, the symmetry is set to the default condition to eliminate its interference when evaluating other FoMs in subsequent simulations.

Ideal linearity implies the same resistance change under the same pulse operation, or in other words, the amount of conductance change induced by the next pulse is independent of the present resistance value. However, in reality, nonlinearity is common in analog RSM devices, which directly impacts neural network learning. In our simulation results, nonlinearity not only affects the recognition accuracy, but also the convergence rate in learning. Fig. 7(b) and (c) illustrates the specific simulation results of these two aspects. As demonstrated in the inset of Fig. 7(c), a large nonlinearity causes the device conductance to be more easily clustered around Gmin as well as $G _ { \mathrm { m a x } }$ and generates an intermediate conductance range whose values are hard to reach, which is detrimental to the neural network weight adjustments. The effect of nonlinearity is similar to asymmetry, but the recognition accuracy degradation is more severe because the conductance of those devices—whose states are near the boundary of the middle conductance range—sometimes repeatedly cross

the boundary. Consequently, such a large nonlinearity leads to poorer convergence efficiency, which means more time, greater energy consumption, and higher device reliability requirements. Therefore, obtaining symmetrical and highly linear devices is critical for realizing efficient and reliable neural network applications.

From the above discussions, it is evident that linearity and symmetry are the most important parameters determining the performance of the neural network system [14]. Many analyses on these two characteristics have been conducted in recent years [104], [115]–[120], and a consensus has emerged that highly linear and symmetric devices are desired for effective learning regardless of the material system or device type. Recently, a major advance in device linearity and symmetry was achieved. Li et al. [121] developed a capacitor-based analog memory device with near-ideal symmetry and linearity, as demonstrated in Fig. 8. Based on this device and the corresponding simulation results, a more rigorous definition of symmetry needs to be considered as the key factor that affects the behavior of analog memory devices for neural network applications. In the traditional view, analog memory devices with a certain degree of nonlinearity usually exhibit concavity in the set process and convexity in the reset process [see the top graph in Fig. 8(b)]. However, the capacitor-based device could present a symmetric but nonlinear curve in both processes and thus lead to an almost identical conductance change (-G) in both directions for the same conductance state. This makes it easier for devices to converge to a specific conductance for an optimal solution rather than to heavily fluctuate in its vicinity. As a result, the recognition accuracy of the neural network improves significantly. In a previous work by Jang et al. [122], a similar simulation and analysis showed the same result. The newly proposed symmetry is rarely observed in RSM devices. It could be regarded as a new direction for device optimization with regard to further research and exploration. It is worth noting that the recent device advance does not imply that nonlinearity becomes

less important. The optimization of linearity and symmetry remains an important goal for analog RSM devices.

b) Dynamic range and level numbers: For the assessment of dynamic range, three different conditions (128 level numbers and nonlinearity of 2, 512 level numbers and nonlinearity of 2, and 512 level numbers and nonlinearity of 5) are considered, as shown in Fig. 9(a). A consistent trend is observed that the recognition accuracy first improves considerably with increase in the dynamic range but then tends to level off. When the dynamic range is larger than 10, the recognition accuracy no longer improves obviously. As mentioned before, analog RSM devices usually have a dynamic range smaller than 10; hence, optimizing this FoM is likely to gain appreciable benefits for the overall performance of the neural networks. When the dynamic range is small, the spacing between different intermediate states is very small. The fluctuation could almost be at the same level as -G. As a result, it is difficult to effectively control the effect of one-time weight update operations as the negative effect of fluctuations becomes more pronounced. Therefore, the recognition accuracy is degraded accordingly. Correspondingly, more learning is needed to make up for this uncontrollability and to achieve a relatively stable convergence. As shown in Fig. 9(b), a small dynamic range causes a low convergence efficiency, while a dynamic range above 10 hardly has any impact. The inset graph shows the effect of dynamic range changes on device characterization under pulse operations, as obtained from the simulations. Thus, the dynamic range should be at least 10 to obtain an acceptable recognition accuracy and convergence efficiency.

In general, level numbers are positively correlated with the recognition accuracy. For the MNIST data set, the statement is verified with high nonlinearity conditions as shown in Fig. 9(c). However, when the nonlinearity is very low, the recognition accuracy does not seem to improve by an increase in level numbers as far as the simulation can

tell. The fact is that the improvement in this situation is concealed by the limitations of the simulation conditions, and the complicated impact of level numbers on the convergence rate should be responsible for it. With excellent linearity, high level numbers introduce more intermediate adjustable states while making the conductance change (-G) during each weight update very small. As a result, the entire synaptic array needs to go through a lengthy iterative process from the initial state to convergence, similar to that in the case of a small fixed learning rate in machine learning. This observation has been verified in subsequent simulations for the cases of high linearity and large level numbers. The results of RCE shown in Fig. 9(d) lead to a similar conclusion. In addition, highly nonlinear devices achieve better convergence efficiency than those with low nonlinearity in the case of high level numbers. This observation seems to contradict the previous discussion on the effect of nonlinearity. However, in fact, it supplements the previous conclusion with a more comprehensive consideration. In detail, when the level numbers are large (>512 total number of available states between $G _ { \mathrm { m i n } }$ and Gmax), a good linearity slows down convergence due to the fixed small learning rate, while high nonlinearity leads to efficiency loss because of the repeated crossing at the borders of the intermediate conductance range, and so moderate nonlinearity can achieve optimal convergence efficiency. In addition, with increasing level numbers, the moderate nonlinearity value corresponding to the optimal convergence efficiency mentioned above also increases.

Since accuracy is positively correlated with the level numbers, there is an inevitable tradeoff between accuracy and convergence efficiency. Considering the energy consumption and performance of current analog RSM devices, it may be a reasonable choice to obtain the highest possible convergence efficiency under the condition of appropriate relaxation of the accuracy requirements. On the other hand, if the device has excellent endurance in intermediate

states, device failure due to repeated operations will not be a concern [123]. In that case, larger level numbers and lower nonlinearity is an effective choice for device optimization for in-memory learning. In addition, if the existing devices can be optimized for linearity and have a linear correlation between -G and the amplitude or width of the operational pulse, both requirements can be satisfied at the same time. In this case, -G of each update is fully controllable. Thus, we can use a high learning rate to accelerate convergence at first and then use a low learning rate to improve accuracy. This method can be easily adapted to various algorithms due to nearly identical hardware and software architectures; however, there are still some difficulties in practical implementation.

In related publications, devices with smaller level numbers (using a total of 64 intermediate states between $G _ { \mathrm { m i n } }$ and $G _ { \mathrm { m a x } } )$ , with a similar definition for the main FoMs, have been explored in the NeuroSim simulator [67], [88]. They have concluded that nonlinearity of 1 and −1 and a dynamic range of 10 are needed to achieve good performance for in-memory learning. This conclusion is also confirmed by our simulation results as shown in Fig. 9(a) and (c). In addition, Fumarola et al. [91] adopted a different definition of nonlinearity, which combines the effects of nonlinearity, level numbers, and asymmetry. The key point is the ratio between the maximum and minimum

conductance change values within the dynamic range under one pulse operation. Based on this, a nonlinearity of less than 0.5 has been proposed for good accuracy. By setting the parameters in our simulation system to those corresponding definitions of nonlinearity in [91], the same result can be reproduced. However, these articles only reveal a small part of the applicable range. A more comprehensive summary is given in Table 5, based on our simulation results. Other published works so far points to a similar conclusion—devices with high linearity, level numbers, symmetry, and a suitable dynamic range are needed for obtaining better cognition accuracy.

c) Fluctuation and variation: In current analog RSM devices, fluctuation is a ubiquitous phenomenon that is widely believed to have an important effect on weight updates during neural network training. In order to evaluate the impact of fluctuations, the conductance change (-G) under ideal pulse operation has been divided into two parts: $\Delta G _ { \mathrm { m e a n } }$ and $\Delta G _ { \mathrm { F l u c t u a t i o n } }$ . The former is usually determined by the nonlinearity, dynamic range, and level numbers, while the latter introduces randomness based on the former so that fluctuation could be considered in each iteration of the in-memory learning process. A normal distribution is selected to characterize this randomness in the simulations and σ representing the standard deviation in the standard normal distribution is used to quantify the degree of fluctuation. As demonstrated in Fig. 10(a), an increase in fluctuation leads to a gradual degradation of the recognition accuracy. When the nonlinearity is very high (>4), the recognition function is no longer reliable. Two conclusions can be derived: 1) accuracy loss becomes worse with increasing fluctuations and 2) the fluctuation tolerance decreases with increasing nonlinearity. From the simulation results, we can conclude that fluctuations lower than 5σ are acceptable for analog RSM devices to avoid obvious accuracy loss.

Because of the randomness in the oxygen vacancy generation position and the size of the conductive filaments in filamentary RRAM devices as well as the difference in diffusion barriers of oxygen vacancies at the interfaces in nonfilamentary devices, device variation is a serious adverse effect that must be considered. The assessment on the impact of device variation is based on the main FoMs of the reference device (including nonlinearity, dynamic range, and level numbers). The variations are assumed to follow a normal distribution, where a random value is applied to all these FoMs so as to characterize one device. The average deviation of all the simulated devices from the reference device indicates the degree of the impact from device variations. The simulation results displayed

in Fig. 10(b) indicate that the accuracy is hardly affected when the device variation is less than 30%.

Since fluctuation and variation are inevitable in analog RSM devices, alleviating their effects is important, for example, by device optimization and by massive or even redundant connections between network nodes to improve the robustness of entire neural networks. However, the advantage of in-memory learning is that it can tolerate certain fluctuations and variations by weakening the impact of individual devices on the overall network. Some research works have already evaluated the variation tolerance capabilities of different neural networks [124], [125]. Querlioz et al. [126] proposed a novel neural network-based computing paradigm, which is a kind of evolution of in-memory unsupervised learning. The system can retain functionality even when the parameters of RSM devices exhibit a relative variation up to 50%. This result further confirms the good tolerance for device variation and the benefit of in-memory learning. Besides the nonuniformity of the devices itself, the effects of nonideal operation environments and operations have also received considerable attention. Kang et al. [127] investigated the time-dependent variability (TDV) and its impact on RRAMbased RSM application. Raghavan et al. [128] analyzed the impact of random telegraph noise (RTN) on read disturbance. Chai et al. [129] explored the irrecoverable degradation of dynamic range induced by the over-reset voltage on the device. Agarwal et al. [119] focused on the impact of read noise and write noise on neural network learning, which is very common in weight updates [130], [131]. To maintain high accuracy (∼1% degradation), the read noise standard deviation should be less than 5% of the dynamic range. The write noise standard deviation should be less than 0.4% of the dynamic range and 300% of a characteristic update (the latter varies slightly depending on the data set and the neural network structure). All these requirements put high demands on the device fabrication and operation environment, which is critical for successful

hardware implementations of neural networks with analog RSM devices.

d) Yield: It corresponds to the proportion of devices that function correctly in terms of resistance switching. Intuitively, the presence of failed devices inevitably leads to a decline in system accuracy. However, interestingly, our simulation shows a somewhat surprising result. As demonstrated in Fig. 10(c), tolerance of a certain degree of device failure is evident in all experiments with different number of hidden-layer neurons. There is no obvious accuracy loss as long as the failure rate is within a certain range (e.g., <20% of 50 hidden neurons and <60% of 200 hidden neurons). This is because in-memory learning can find a near-optimal solution and adjust the configuration of neural networks to compensate for the adverse effects caused by some failed devices. This further attests to the importance of pursuing in-memory learning. Note that too few hidden neurons would result in an increase in accuracy loss (e.g., ∼10% accuracy loss with 50 hidden neurons and ∼3% with 200 hidden neurons). Without a sufficient number of effective feature extractions, it is hard to obtain good accuracy. Such a low yield requirement is shown by the simulation results of a relatively simple MNIST recognition task. Though higher yields may be needed for more complex neural network applications, our study provides insights and reveals important advantages of inmemory learning.

Based on the simulation results discussed above, the requirements of Level 1 FoMs for neuromorphic computing with in-memory learning are summarized in Table 5. Although the results in the table are obtained via the simulation of a small neural network, they still provide an important reference for larger scale and more complicated networks. The results are determined based on comprehensive considerations of accuracy loss and RCE. For conditions of: 1) nonlinearity lower than 3 in the set process and higher than −3 in the reset process; 2) level numbers between 128 and 1024; and 3) dynamic range higher than 10, an acceptable reliability (lower than 5% accuracy loss) and efficiency (higher than 50% RCE) are guaranteed (some other representative conditions are also summarized in Table 5). The symmetry should be smaller than 1 to ensure an accuracy loss of no more than 1% within the range of the evaluated nonlinearity. The fluctuation and variation should be lower than 5σ and 30%, respectively, to avoid obvious accuracy loss. The requirement of device yield depends on specific applications and neural networks. For the simple MNIST application with a two-layer perceptron, a device yield larger than 40% is sufficient using in-memory learning methods. What needs to be emphasized here is that all the results are based on the reference device (symmetry, nonlinearity of 2 and −2, dynamic range of 10 and 128 level numbers). Therefore, some other situations with affordable accuracy loss and convergence efficiency (but not in the range of the table) are also possible. However, this exactly reflects the tradeoff among the different FoMs.

From the perspective of the factors affecting the neural network performance, approaches to improve device performance essentially include the following three aspects: lowering the difference of -G in both set and reset operations for the same conductance state, lowering the difference of -G in the same set/reset operation at different conductance states, and lowering the maximum of -G. Further goals of the device optimization include controlling the conductance adjustments so that they are close to the ideal ones obtained from the software learning rates. The smaller the gap between these two, the better the accuracy and convergence efficiency that can be achieved with in-memory learning analog neural networks.

2) Level 2 FoMs: If Level 1 FoMs determine the function and accuracy of the in-memory learning system, Level 2 FoMs determine the scope of suitable applications for a RSM device and whether the hardware implementation based on the device is cost-effective. Among the Level 2 FoMs, endurance and retention dictate the device reliability, while device size, operational voltage, speed, and compatibility with the CMOS fabricating process determine the economic benefits of large-scale in-memory learning systems.

a) Retention: Human memory is an intelligent system that is weakly dependent on the plasticity of individual neurons or synapses. However, in a neuromorphic system, a certain amount of stable plasticity is required for artificial synapses to ensure enough data retention [132]. Regarding nonvolatile digital memory, longer than tenyear retention is widely required. In contrast, the retention requirement for neuromorphic computing can be application-dependent due to their diversity and complexity. Despite this, there is no doubt that longer retention time would help both the synaptic weight update and the inference after learning and avoid adverse effects caused by serious conductance drift.

Yakopcic et al. [48] selected eight resistance states of a $\mathrm { T a O } _ { x } / \mathrm { H f A l } _ { y } 0 _ { z }$ analog RRAM device to investigate its retention characteristics. Through statistical measurements of 1k devices, it is observed that different intermediate resistance states exhibit normal distributions. It is also found that the standard deviation of the distribution increases gradually over time. These observations provide valuable input for the statistic modeling of the retention characteristics of analog RSM devices and will be helpful for further analysis of the impact of retention on the performance of neural networks. In addition, long retention times could also relieve the requirements on other FoMs to some extent. For example, if there is no need to add new categories or recognition features, a good retention of neural network weights will ensure the network to perform correctly for a long time without additional updates. Then both endurance and energy consumption will benefit from this.

b) Endurance: For artificial synapses, certain amount of endurance is required to accommodate the persistent

weight updating activities. In the early exploration of memory applications using the emerging nonvolatile devices, considerable research has been devoted to studying endurance over the entire dynamic range. Wong et al. [33] analyzed the endurance degradation caused by the large number of defects and oxygen vacancies. Lee et al. [133] and Chen et al. [134] discussed the impact of the generation of an interface layer between the oxide and the electrode on endurance. Since switching in the intermediate state is a critical physical process during in-memory learning operations, research on this type of endurance (focusing on intermediate states) has also made some progress recently. Under weak weight update pulses, the intermediate state switching cycles of analog RSM devices can be increased by more than five orders of magnitude compared with full window switching under strong programming pulses. A $1 0 ^ { 1 1 }$ -cycle endurance is proved to be sufficient for in-memory learning for various data sets (from MNIST to ImageNet) [135]. However, the degradation of other FoMs, such as linearity and dynamic range, are worth paying close attention, which may influence the learning accuracy of the neural network when it is retrained with new data sets [136].

c) Area, speed, and energy consumption: The ideal unit size of RSM devices is $4 \mathrm { F } ^ { 2 }$ (1F device length and width with 1F spacing between devices). Some designs use an additional transistor (e.g., 1T1R structure, $6 { - } 1 2 \mathrm { F } ^ { 2 } )$ to eliminate the sneak path, thereby reducing this size advantage. However, such a cell size is still considerably smaller than traditional synapses (e.g., SRAM, 120–200F2, DRAM, $6 0 { - } 1 0 0 \mathrm { F } ^ { 2 }$ [137]) realized by multiple transistors, capacitors, or complex circuits.

Speed is an important indicator for the operation efficiency, which directly determines the application complexity that can be tolerated. The human brain can easily process complex tasks based on processing elements with an average frequency of ∼10 Hz. The efficiency of the human brain is, due to its huge number of processing elements $( \mathrm { i . e . , } \sim 1 0 ^ { 1 1 }$ neurons and ${ \sim } 1 0 ^ { 1 5 }$ synapses), millions of times larger than that found in current ANNs. For emerging applications, such as autonomous vehicles, processing elements, and synapses, that operate at higher speed or at smaller delays it, is highly desirable to compensate for the limited number of processing elements. Most analog RSM devices have response times as short as a few nanoseconds (except interfacial switching RRAMs, for which the response time is in the range of millisecond) and are comparable to DRAMs. Such a high operating speed as well as storage and computing capability make RRAMs have a competitive candidate compared with existing CMOS circuits for many applications.

Energy consumption mainly occurs in the programming process of the weight updates. Existing devices show a programming energy in the pJ range (e.g., for RRAMs, it is typically in the range of 100 fJ–10 pJ, and for PCM, it is around 10–100 pJ). Some methods have been proposed to further reduce the energy consumption such as putting

the devices in series with a selector or a transistor, using a relatively higher LRS, and so on. However, in all these cases, some other FoMs are sacrificed, such as area and linearity. Therefore, the tradeoffs need to be considered carefully.

In conclusion, the area, speed, and energy consumption are generally the main FoMs to evaluate the cost and efficiency of the implementation of specific functions and applications. Compared with traditional CMOS implementations, analog RSM devices have unparalleled advantages in these aspects, which is also one of the important reasons why they have gained such a wide attention. Nevertheless, more research is needed to further reduce the area, increase the speed, and reduce the energy consumption.

# A. Current Status of Analog RSM Devices

To obtain an intuitive understanding of the characteristics of current analog RSM devices, a summary of device characteristics (based on the definitions given in Table 3) is given in Table 6, allowing a quantitative comparison. 4) Codesign of Cell Structure and Programming Scheme: Codesign of a cell structure and the programming scheme is another important method to achieve linear and symmetric conductance tuning. For example, the 1T1R configuration with $\mathrm { H f O } _ { 2 }$ -based RRAMs [146] has provided a practical solution by ingeniously using the current compliance of the transistor. In contrast to the programming schemes mentioned earlier, this scheme does not vary the pulse applied to the RRAM device; instead, it varies the pulse applied to the gate of the transistor. First, a sufficiently large positive pulse is applied to the RRAM bottom electrode to initialize the state; next, the gate voltage to set the device is set to the desired conductance level by using another positive pulse to the top electrode. As a result, the device conductance can be effectively controlled in both set and reset processes by specifying the compliance current. Thanks to the maturity of the transistor process, this method could also help alleviate device variations and fluctuations.

The methods discussed above explore the relationship between the programming pulses and the conductance tuning curves using different schemes (e.g., voltage pulsewidth, amplitude, a combination of both, or current pulse). As the resistance increases or decreases, the energy applied to the device varies nonlinearly when using identical pulse schemes, which makes it difficult to keep -G constant at different conductance states. In addition, the carrier conduction mechanisms in analog RSM devices, such as the Schottky emission and the Poole–Frankel effect, exhibit a nonlinear I–V relationship, which makes this phenomenon of $\Delta G$ variation more problematic. However, as discussed before, when one applies varying programming schemes, the nonlinear designs in amplitude or width of the operational pulses and energy tuning in current pulses can reduce these phenomena. The excellent FoMs of the device in [146] further confirm the effectiveness of a carefully chosen voltage scheme for improving the device characteristics. Codesigns of cell structures and programming schemes will become one of the key approaches for future device optimization.

# C. Optimization Methods for Material and Device Structures

Although the programming scheme provides a promising solution, it still has several limitations such as the

difficulty to precisely control the local filament, complex peripheral circuits to drive the synapses [57], and a readbefore-write step in some cases that makes it hard to support parallel operations. These limitations seriously influence the reliability, energy consumption, and learning efficiency of the hardware implementations of neural networks. Optimizing the material and structure of the device itself can offer another alternative. In this section, some device optimizations and their underlying mechanisms are discussed, including electrothermal modulation [54], [141], [145], preferable locations of conductive filaments [147], interface barrier modulation [148], and optimization of cell structure [143]. Linearity [143], [145], level numbers [54], [139], [141], [147], dynamic range [143], retention [148], and variation [147] have been improved in these devices. Due to the complex and diverse material systems, it is hard to derive a generic optimization method. Therefore, the design ideas and an understanding of the underlying mechanisms in existing works are more useful for further exploration and research on device optimizations.

1) Electrothermal Modulation: Inspired by the optimization of the programming schemes, there have been extensive efforts to study the effect of electric field in the device operation. The thermal effect, which is usually associated with the electric field, has also received considerable attention. The most common method is to insert an electrothermal modulation layer in the device. With proper material selection, such layers can help modulate the electric field and improve the heat distribution, thereby achieving the purpose of device optimization.

Let us consider the RRAM based on T $\mathrm { \dot { a } _ { 2 } O _ { 5 } / T i O } _ { x }$ material stack [141] as an example. In such a system, after a forming process with abrupt switching behavior, the conductive filament is created only in the thin $\mathrm { T a } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 5 }$ layer, whereas the overlying $\mathrm { T i O } _ { x }$ acts as a reservoir for oxygen ions [153], [154]. The oxygen ions diffuse as well as migrate between the $\mathrm { T i O } _ { x }$ layer and the $\mathrm { T a } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 5 }$ layer under the control of the electric field. However, this is a slower and more random process due to the large activation energy (∼1.2 eV [155]) and the smaller field acceleration factor in the $\mathrm { T a } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 5 }$ layer (the reduced dependence on the applied field makes it easier for oxygen ions to move radially when driven by the local temperature and mutual Coulomb repulsion [156]). Based on these microscopic materialrelated aspects, a more gradual and uniform oxidation of the conductive filament could be achieved. Thus, when the voltage is applied on the formed device, the electric field is relatively uniform across the remaining oxide region (where a conductive filament does not exist) and the oxygen vacancy is generated preferentially in the surroundings of the conductive filament (where the local temperature is higher). This mechanism can give the device an analog switching behavior along with the width modulation of the conductive filament induced by the oxygen vacancy generation and annihilation. $\operatorname { I t } ,$ thus, provides a method

for improving the level numbers by the electrothermal effect.

Another representative method is based on the thermal enhanced layer (TEL)/HfOx material system [54]. Experimental results have confirmed that HfOx-based RRAMs can transit from abrupt switching to analog switching at a high temperature $( \sim 1 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ . In general, conductive filaments are more likely to be produced at places with a higher temperature. However, if the local high temperature can spread and cause a more uniform temperature distribution, it is very likely to simultaneously generate multiple conductive filaments and realize analog switching behavior. Based on these considerations, a TEL was inserted between the resistive switching layer and the top electrode to confine the heat distribution, thereby achieving the goal of realizing analog switching device characteristics at room temperature. In the subsequent work [145], an electrothermal modulation method was developed to further improve the linearity of the conductance tuning. The nonlinearity in this kind of RRAM devices originates from the transformation of resistive switching mechanisms in different conductance intervals as shown in Fig. 12(a). In the reset process, as the current gradually decreases and the conductive filament gap increases, it becomes more difficult for vacancies to migrate due to the reduced local temperature and electric field, which results in a gradual decrease in -G as it gets closer to $G _ { \mathrm { m i n } }$ . In the set process, the conductive filament experiences two stages: filament formation and thickening. The difference in conductance change rate at these two stages directly causes an intrinsic nonlinearity. Therefore, in order to solve the nonlinearity problem in such devices, it is necessary to make the conductance change rate at different stages as close as possible. An electrothermal modulation layer is designed to cap the switching layer and to promote the formation of uniform vacancy distribution by low thermal conductivity [157], [158] (suppressing the impact of filament connection in the set process via multiweak filaments) and modulate the electric field changing rate in the conductive filament gap region. Using this approach, a $\mathrm { H f O } _ { x }$ -based RRAM

with good linearity in both operation directions and a 10× dynamic range was demonstrated [145].

2) Preferable Locations of Conductive Filaments: The uncertainty of the formation position of conductive filaments in the analog RSM device leads to a large variability. Thus, how to define the preferable locations of conductive filaments during the device fabrication stage or operation stage becomes a critical problem for the research community. A material and structure-dependent method that confines metal filaments in a predefined 1-D channel to mitigate the device variability issue has been developed in recent years [147]. Traditional amorphous-phase binaryoxides-based RRAM suffers unavoidable variations and fluctuations due to the uncontrollable filament dynamics in the amorphous solid [159], [160]. To solve this problem, this method used a single-crystalline SiGe layer epitaxially grown on Si as the switching medium and utilized the threading dislocations in SiGe to provide preferential diffusion paths. A conceptual schematic of the device during switching is displayed in Fig. 12(b). The cross-sectional transmission electron microscope (TEM) image confirms the existence of threading dislocations [see Fig. 12(c)]. By combining the immiscible active electrode (Ag) [161], the device effectively avoids the randomness of the location of the conductive filament generation and thereby exhibits excellent temporal (cycle-to-cycle, 1%) and spatial (device-to-device, 4.9%) uniformity. Further experimental results show that this is still valid for nanoscale devices and the channel size can be adjusted via Schimmel etching technology. This selective etching method gives the threading dislocations more space to accommodate the silver atoms. As a result, a very high ON/OFF ratio of more than 100 with 200 set and reset pulses was realized.

3) Interface Barrier Modulation: Compared to the filamentary devices, interface-switching-based devices inherently show an analog resistive switching behavior. This is due to the change in the barrier height or doping concentration caused by the redox reaction and oxygen vacancies or the simultaneous migration of ions over the

entire interface area. However, it is difficult to obtain long retention times for these devices because of the easily occurring diffusion and spontaneous oxidation after removing the applied voltage. These phenomena cause the accuracy of neural networks to fall sharply when the ratio between the device retention time and the duration of each learning operation falls below ∼10 000 [91]. Therefore, how to improve retention becomes a critical problem for interface switching-based devices.

Interface barrier modulation is one useful method to optimize the performance of such kind of device since its working mechanism depends on the interface. A representative device is the $\mathrm { P r _ { 0 . 7 } C a _ { 0 . 3 } M n O _ { 3 } ( P C M O ) }$ -based structure. By introducing a $\mathrm { M o O } _ { x }$ buffer layer together with a reactive Al electrode to the PCMO devices, both retention and dynamic range are improved [148]. The $\mathsf { M o O } _ { x }$ layer exhibits unique multivalent oxidation states with different conductance values [162]. A $\mathsf { M o O } _ { x }$ buffer layer with suitable stoichiometry could have a higher energy barrier so that spontaneous oxidation between the electrode and PCMO can be suppressed, which leads to a longer retention time. In addition, the Al layer acting as the reactive metal could help improve the conductance contrast due to the large difference in conductivity between Al and its oxides.

4) Optimization of Cell Structure: In addition to improvements in material selection and device fabrication processes, the optimization of the cell structure can also greatly improve the device characteristics. A new structure with one-transistor/two-resistor (1T2R) has been proposed as an alternative cell for storing the weight. Unlike the traditional 1T1R structure, the MOSFET, which is connected in parallel with the resistor and the RRAM, works in the steep subthreshold region as shown in Fig. 13(a). A small change in the voltage drop on the RRAM directly controls the gate bias of the MOSFET, thereby causing a large change in the drain current, which can significantly improve the dynamic range $\left( > 1 0 0 \times \right)$ . The series resistor, which acts as a voltage divider with the RRAM device, is used to convert identical input voltage pulses into incremental pulses to apply on the RRAM device, as illustrated

in Fig. 13(b). This new design improves the linearity of the conductance changes, as shown in Fig. 13(c).

The research efforts discussed above have achieved considerable improvements in the FoMs of analog RSM devices either by material or by device structure designs. However, there are still significant barriers to overcome before achieving the desired device characteristics that are suitable for large-scale analog neural network implementations. For now, algorithms, circuits, and devices will need to adapt to each other in order to effectively take advantages of analog RSM devices. The demand of specific applications is very likely to require more diversified optimization directions, which are interesting challenges for the research community.

# V. C O N C L U S I O N A N D O U T L O O K

Thus far, considerable progress has been made (from the device level up to the system level) in analog RSM devices for in-memory learning applications. Yet, considerable design challenges remain at each level due to device limitations, parasitic effects of crossbar arrays, immature mapping algorithms and hardware, lack of efficient customized peripheral circuits, and so on. These issues will become even more serious when the array size is scaled up for practical applications [163]. A summary and outlook of the various limitations is given below.

At the device level, several FoMs, such as linearity, level numbers, dynamic range, variation, and fluctuation, still need to improve significantly. Although changing the programming schemes can alleviate these deficiencies, the read-before-write step, the lack of parallel operation, and the complex control circuits make it difficult to implement and greatly reduce its effectiveness.

At the array level, sneak path currents and parasitic resistance seriously constrain the size of the arrays. The former can be potentially solved by 1T1R or 1S1R designs. However, the cell area is sacrificed when using the 1T1R design. Until now, there has not been a reliable analog 1S1R array demonstration. Parasitic resistance inevitably causes a sizable IR drop. A higher resistance of LRS is

considered to reduce the IR drop, but the high resistance range of RSM devices usually exhibits poor analog switching characteristics.

At the circuit level, since processors typically work in the digital domain while RSM devices generate analog signals, communication between the two requires a large number of ADCs, DACs, or other conversion circuits. The area and energy consumption of these circuits can far exceed those of analog RSM devices, which hugely weakens the inherent advantages of using these devices. Many arithmetic operations, such as copying and shifting of weights, are still hard to efficiently realize since no good circuit solutions exist. Codesign of device and circuits is needed to overcome these limitations.

At the algorithm level, most of the algorithms that have been adopted for hardware implementations are designed for digital memory devices. Although there are some algorithms that give promising results for analog switching device implementations, such as BP and Manhattan update rule, they are not yet able to fully adapt to the characteristics of analog RSM devices. Chang et al. [164] developed a hardware-applicable deep-learning algorithm that improves the classification accuracy of MNIST handwritten digital data set to 94.8% even with extremely high asymmetric nonlinearity. Accurate in-memory learning can be realized using RRAMs with small level numbers by employing a hybrid-weight network that combines the internal analog and external binary weights [165]. Such designs can take advantage of the current devices and circumvent the effects of nonideal factors. In this way, the functional correctness of the hardware implementation can be guaranteed. The success in exploring algorithms for analog RSM devices further highlights the importance of suitable algorithms. The co-optimization of the device and algorithm is believed to be a key to future hardware implementations of deep neural networks.

At the system level, the lack of effective electronic design automation (EDA) tools for analog RSM devices has seriously limited the systematic simulation of large systems. This has hindered the design and implementation of complex systems. In addition, it has been hard for hardware implementations to achieve accuracies that are comparable with software-based systems. The nonideality of devices and imperfect system designs are the main causes of inferior performance. Ambrogio et al. [166]

developed a mixed hardware–software neural network. Accuracies equivalent to those of software-based learning on various commonly used machine-learning test data sets were achieved by combining long-term storage in PCM, nearly linear updates in volatile capacitors, and weightdata transfer with “polarity inversion” to cancel out the inherent device-to-device variations. Although they only provided a solution for fully connected neural-network layers, the result reveals the potential of system-level optimizations.

As device process technology and theory become more and more mature, further enhancements in device characteristics are expected. Better linearity, dynamic range, level numbers, endurance with less variations, fewer fluctuations, and longer retention time need to be pursued. Furthermore, new programming schemes and device structures are expected to be developed to reduce the parasitic effects. These advancements will further promote the innovation in algorithms as well as architectures and ultimately achieve the goal of efficient in-memory learning based on analog RSM devices and some applicationspecific or general-purpose chips designs. For example, Li et al. [146] monolithically integrated hafnium oxidebased memristors into a multiple-layer neural network and experimentally demonstrated in situ learning capability with a standard machine learning data set. This work further confirms the feasibility of in-memory learning for hardware implementation, which was inconceivable a few years ago. Furthermore, with the continued optimization of device characteristics, new applications are also likely to emerge. Some promising works have already begun to show this trend and potential, for example, artificial neuron [167], general partial differential equation solver [168], analog signal and image processing [147], pattern classification with unsupervised learning [78], [148], [169], artificial nociceptors [170], reinforcement learning [171], and brain–chip interfaces [172]. There is no doubt that the applications of analog RSM devices will become more abundant as more research and explorations are being done. The corresponding peripheral circuit designs, mapping methods, and EDA tools will also prosper. We expect that breakthroughs in dedicated chip demonstrations for in-memory learning and other real-world applications based on analog RSM devices will materialize in the near future through close collaboration among multiple research fields. ■
