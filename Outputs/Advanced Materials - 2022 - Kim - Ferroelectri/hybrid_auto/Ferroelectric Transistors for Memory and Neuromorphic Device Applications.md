# Ferroelectric Transistors for Memory and Neuromorphic Device Applications

Ik-Jyae Kim and Jang-Sik Lee*

Ferroelectric materials have been intensively investigated for highperformance nonvolatile memory devices in the past decades, owing to their nonvolatile polarization characteristics. Ferroelectric memory devices are expected to exhibit lower power consumption and higher speed than conventional memory devices. However, non-complementary metal–oxide– semiconductor (CMOS) compatibility and degradation due to fatigue of traditional perovskite-based ferroelectric materials have hindered the development of high-density and high-performance ferroelectric memories in the past. The recently developed hafnia-based ferroelectric materials have attracted immense attention in the development of advanced semiconductor devices. Because hafnia is typically used in CMOS processes, it can be directly incorporated into current semiconductor technologies. Additionally, hafnia-based ferroelectrics show high scalability and large coercive fields that are advantageous for high-density memory devices. This review summarizes the recent developments in ferroelectric devices, especially ferroelectric transistors, for next-generation memory and neuromorphic applications. First, the types of ferroelectric memories and their operation mechanisms are reviewed. Then, issues limiting the realization of high-performance ferroelectric transistors and possible solutions are discussed. The experimental demonstration of ferroelectric transistor arrays, including 3D ferroelectric NAND and its operation characteristics, are also reviewed. Finally, challenges and strategies toward the development of next-generation memory and neuromorphic applications based on ferroelectric transistors are outlined.

# 2. Ferroelectric Materials and Their Memory Applications

# 2.1. Properties of Ferroelectric Materials

Ferroelectric materials are dielectric materials with permanent polarization; the application of an external electric field can switch the dipole direction in such materials.[27] Among the 32  crystal classes of materials, 21  exhibit non-centrosymmetric structure. Among these non-centrosymmetric crystal classes, 20  exhibit piezoelectricity, with 10  crystal classes exhibiting spontaneous temperature-dependent polarization (pyroelectricity).[28] Some pyroelectric materials show spontaneous external-electric-field-dependent polarization (ferroelectricity),[28,29] with a hysteresis loop in the polarization versus electric-field curve (Figure 1a).[11] Several important parameters can be determined from the hysteresis loop of ferroelectric materials. Saturation polarization $( P _ { \mathrm { { s } } } )$ , remnant polarization $( P _ { \mathrm { r } } )$ , and $E _ { \mathrm { c } }$ are characteristic parameters of ferroelectric materials.[28] The $P _ { \mathrm { s } }$ and $P _ { \mathrm r }$ values indicate the maximum polarization under an external electric field and polarization after the removal of the external electric field, respectively, while $E _ { \mathrm { c } }$ indicates the electric field required for polarization switching. In ferroelectric materials, polarization switching occurs through the nucleation and growth of ferroelectric domains.[4] Ferroelectric materials generally exhibit a perovskite structure (with an $\mathrm { A B O } _ { 3 }$ chemical formula,[1] where the A and B atoms are cations). The A, B, and oxygen atoms are positioned at the corners, body center, and face centers of the crystal, respectively (Figure 1b). The position of the B atom is shifted on applying an external electric field, forming a dipole moment due to unbalanced electrical charges. The B atom generally exhibits two stable positions (with minimum free energy values) under an external electric field, representing the stable polarization states of ferroelectric materials (Figure  1c). Because the polarization reversal process is driven by the electric field and is nonvolatile, ferroelectric memory is considered a promising candidate for nonvolatile memory with stable memory states.[30]

# 2.2. Types of Ferroelectric Memories

In the one-transistor–one-capacitor (1T–1C) structured ferroelectric random-access memory (FeRAM), a ferroelectric capacitor is used as a charge-storage element.[18] Memory state is stored in the ferroelectric capacitor, and the read operation is carried out by measuring its output current (Figure 2a). The cell design of the FeRAM is similar to that of the dynamic random-access memory (DRAM) (utilizing one access transistor and one capacitor). Dielectric materials are used in

DRAM cell capacitors, whereas ferroelectric materials (such as PZT) are used in FeRAM cell capacitors. In FeRAMs, the write operation is conducted by switching the polarization of the ferroelectric layer in the capacitor.[30] For a read operation, the access transistor switches the polarization of the ferroelectric capacitor to a particular state. During switching process, the current is measured to determine the state of ferroelectric capacitor. For example, if the ferroelectric capacitor is programmed to the same state, only small current is observed. However, if the ferroelectric capacitor is programmed to a different state, polarization switching generates a large output current.[30] As the read process overwrites the state of the cell, it is a destructive process that requires re-programming. The nonvolatile nature of ferroelectric polarization makes the FeRAM more advantageous to use than the DRAM. In DRAMs, each cell should be re-programmed after short periods of time by the refresh process due to charge leakage. Therefore, most of the power consumed in DRAMs is used for the refresh process. Contrarily, due to the nonvolatile nature

of ferroelectric polarization, the FeRAM does not require the refresh process, reducing its power consumption. In 1990s, the first commercial FeRAM product was fabricated.[25,31] However, after the 2000s, FeRAMs have only been utilized for specialized purposes due to their technological issues (which will be discussed in Section 3.1). Another type of ferroelectric memory is the FTJ.[18,32–34] FTJ and ferroelectric capacitors exhibit similar structures, with an extremely thin ferroelectric layer used in the FTJ to facilitate the tunneling of charge carriers through the ferroelectric layer (Figure 2b). The accumulation or depletion of charge carriers at the ferroelectric layer-electrode interface depends on the polarization state of the ferroelectric layer.[30] Additionally, polarization switching modifies the tunneling barrier height for charge carriers, changing the FTJ resistance.[32] The ferroelectric layer is also used as a gate insulating layer for transistors. For ferroelectric memory with a one-transistor structure (i.e., a ferroelectric transistor), the gate insulating layer is replaced with a ferroelectric material (Figure  2c).[17,18] The application of a gate voltage exceeding the coercive voltage modifies its polarization state, modulating the channel conductance by the accumulation or depletion of charge carriers. # 3. Ferroelectric Memories

# 3.1. History of Ferroelectric Memories

Ferroelectric materials and their applications have been extensively investigated since the discovery of ferroelectricity in BTO in the 1940s.[38,39] Ferroelectricity was discovered in the PZT system in the 1950s.[6,9] BTO and PZT exhibit similar perovskite crystal structures.[6,40] The concept of ferroelectric memories in 1T–1C type FeRAMs and ferroelectric transistors have been reported.[38,41–43] After rigorous research on ferroelectric materials and their applications, ferroelectric memory products were commercialized in the 1990s (Figure 3a).[31,44,45] However, the main limitations of PZT (process complexity, non-CMOS compatibility, and polarization degradation after numerous

operation cycles [ fatigue]) require resolution before the development of high-performance ferroelectric memories. Thus, numerous studies on high-performance ferroelectric materials have been subsequently conducted (Figure 3b). Layered perovskites, such as SBT, was discovered in the 1990s;[46] the crystal structures of these materials contain oxide layers between perovskite layers. Despite their fatigue-free properties,[7,46–49] these materials do not overcome the non-CMOS compatibility of layered perovskites (due to complex crystal structure and process requirements), thereby hindering the development of high-density ferroelectric memory (Figure  3c,d).[10] Furthermore, nonvolatile ferroelectric transistors using SBT utilize a thick ferroelectric layer for a reasonable memory window. Oxide electrodes (such as IrO  and RuO ) overcome the issue of high fatigue in PZT;[9,47] however, high annealing temperatures, noble-material requirement, and the elemental diffusion of Pb make their utilization in CMOS processes challenging.[2,50] Thus, complex processes, such as integrating oxide electrodes and barrier layers to protect the ferroelectric from hydrogeninduced degradation, are required to utilize perovskite-based ferroelectrics in semiconductor technologies. Additionally, the formation of an interfacial layer during deposition and annealing causes memory-window and endurance-property

degradations.[51] These issues, along with the thickness limitations of PZT, have enabled the utilization of ferroelectric devices primarily in large technology nodes, and 3D integration was unimaginable. After the discovery of CMOS-compatible silicon-doped hafnia in 2011, its ferroelectricity has been used for fabricating ferroelectric transistors with promising properties.[19,20,52,53] The effect of elemental doping on ferroelectricity has also been investigated.[2,54,55] Additionally, ferroelectric memory devices containing the 1T–1C FeRAM, FTJs, and ferroelectric transistors have been intensively investigated.[22,24,25,56–60] Furthermore, the facile deposition of hafnia-based ferroelectrics using ALD has been used to fabricate next-generation 3D memory devices based on ferroelectric hafnia.[23,26,61,62]

# 3.2. Hafnia-Based Ferroelectrics

In CMOS processes, hafnia is a promising alternative among high-k materials to replace $\mathrm { S i O } _ { 2 }$ in transistors. Hafnia, which has a large bandgap, can be directly deposited on Si with stable characteristics; it exhibits the monoclinic, tetragonal, orthorhombic, and cubic phases (Figure 4a).[2,3,63–65] In bulk hafnia, the monoclinic phase is usually observed at room temperature, whereas tetragonal and cubic phases are observed at elevated temperatures (tetragonal: 1973  K, cubic: 2773  K). The ferroelectricity in hafnia is attributed to its orthorhombic phase, which is challenging to observe in the bulk material, and requires a modulation of the stress, grain size, and doping elementconcentration.[2,63,66] Si is the first dopant element reported to induce ferroelectricity in hafnia; a very small percentage of the dopant element  is required.[19] After extensive research on dopant elements and their influence on ferroelectric properties, other dopants (such as Zr, Y, and Al) have also been used for hafnia-based ferroelectrics.[54,55,67–69] Numerous studies indicate that ferroelectric characteristics can be engineered by controlling the dopant concentration (Figure  4b).[54,55] Hafniabased ferroelectrics show high remnant polarization and can be scaled down to achieve a thickness of a few nanometers having ferroelectricity (Figure  4c).[16] The ferroelectricity of hafniabased ferroelectrics with thickness values ranging from 1  nm to a few nanometers have been analyzed.[70–72] Recent studies indicate that thin films of undoped hafnia and zirconia exhibit ferroelectricity under optimized processing conditions.[73,74] Analyzing the properties of ferroelectric materials under the repeated application of an external electric field is important. Usually, the neighboring metal layers (such as gate electrodes) scavenge oxygen from the hafnia-based ferroelectric layer, stabilizing the orthorhombic phase.[75–77] Under repeated external electric-field cycles, oxygen vacancies within the ferroelectric layer are redistributed, unpinning the ferroelectric domains and thereby transforming the ferroelectric orthorhombic phase. These changes increase $P _ { \mathrm r }$ and wake-up. Diverse explanations are also being proposed to explain the mechanism of wake-up phenomena in hafnia-based ferroelectrics. First, the redistribution of defects such as oxygen vacancies can reduce the internal bias fields, assist domain wall depinning, and stabilize the ferroelectric orthorhombic phase in the hafnia layer.[78,79] Moreover, electric-field-induced crystallization to the ferroelectric orthorhombic phase was suggested as the mechanism for the

wake-up process.[80] Finally, ferroelastic switching from out-ofplane texture to in-plane orientation was proposed.[65,81] A significant number of external electric-field cycles cause domain pinning due to defect generation within the ferroelectric layer, and transform the monoclinic phase.[82,83] These changes reduce the $P _ { \mathrm r }$ value and cause fatigue.

# 3.3. Hafnia-Based Ferroelectric Transistors

Ferroelectric transistors are fabricated by integrating ferroelectric layers in gate insulating layers of transistors. Perovskite-based ferroelectric materials, which typically show small $E _ { \mathrm { c } } ,$ require several hundred nanometer-thick films to acquire a reasonable memory window (Figure  4d).[16,18] By contrast, hafnia-based ferroelectrics, show higher $E _ { \mathrm { c } }$ and require thinner films for a similar memory window, thereby facilitating scalability.[22,59] Therefore, hafnia-based ferroelectric materials have been used to fabricate ferroelectric transistors immediately after their discovery.[20,52,86]

However, similar to perovskite-based ferroelectrics, hafniabased ferroelectrics form interfacial layers between the ferroelectric layer and Si-based channel materials (Figure 5a).[9,87,88] These interfacial layers are formed during the deposition of hafnia-based ferroelectrics and annealing process. Because annealing is necessary to obtain ferroelectricity in hafniabased ferroelectrics, the formation of an interfacial layer is challenging to avoid. These layers significantly increase operation voltage (due to voltage drop through them), exhibiting a lower dielectric constant than that of hafnia-based ferroelectrics (Figure 5b).[87] Moreover, the endurance characteristics are limited due to the charge-trapping and breakdown of the interfacial layer.[89,90] To overcome this limitation, diverse methods such as incorporating a $\mathrm { L a } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O } _ { 3 }$ layer in Zr-doped hafnia or utilization of an optimized voltage pulses have been investigated.[91,92] Similar to that in perovskite-based ferroelectrics, the insertion of an additional insulating layer between the ferroelectric and channel layers can increase the endurance characteristics. Furthermore, endurance characteristics are improved using a metal–ferroelectric–insulator–semiconductor gate stack.[88,93–96] Most studies have incorporated high-k insulating layers instead of low-k interfacial layers that reduce the switching voltage and electrical stress in the interfacial layer (Figure 5c). Among the diverse insulating layers reported, zirconia enhances the endurance properties and ferroelectricity of Zr-doped hafnia (Figure  5d).[95,97] The additional zirconia layer acts not only as an insulating layer but also as a seed layer for the crystallization of hafnia-based ferroelectrics, thereby improving the crystalline quality of the hafnia and enhancing the endurance and memory-window characteristics of the ferroelectric transistor. Additionally, interface traps generated after program and erase cycles are suppressed using zirconia (Figure 5e). Further research and optimizations are required on the endurance enhancement of ferroelectric transistors.

Oxide-semiconductor-based channel materials have been used to overcome the limitations of interfacial layers.[23,98–100] Oxide semiconductor channels, such as $\mathrm { I n G a Z n O } _ { x } ,$ InZnO , and $\mathrm { I n Z n S n O } _ { x } ,$ improve the endurance characteristics of ferroelectric transistors. Numerous experimental studies on

oxide-semiconductor-based high-performance ferroelectric transistors have been published (Figure 6a).[23,98–100] An oxidesemiconductor-based ferroelectric transistor has been fabricated using the ferroelectric $\mathrm { H f Z r O } _ { x }$ and oxide semiconductor

$\mathrm { I n G a Z n O } _ { x }$ as the gate insulator and channel, respectively. The $\mathrm { I n G a Z n O } _ { x }$ -based ferroelectric transistor shows stable multilevel characteristics owing to controlled partial polarization in its ferroelectric layer (Figure  6b). At the erased state, the $\mathrm { H f Z r O } _ { x }$

layer is in a downward polarization direction, which depletes the electrons, causing a low channel conductance (Figure  6c). Upon applying programming pulses with increasing pulse amplitudes, the direction of the polarization gradually changes from downwards to upwards, causing an accumulation of electrons at the interface of the ferroelectric and channel layers and thus increasing the conductance. Additionally, owing to absence of an interfacial layer, the ferroelectric transistor shows stable and reliable switching characteristics with a small cycle-to-cycle variation.[99] Interfacial-layer formation during the annealing process can be suppressed by using oxide semiconductors as channel materials (Figure  6d).[101] An absence of the interfacial layer leads to high endurance due to the suppression of chargetrapping. Also, low operation voltage can be achieved because

the voltage loss due to low-k interfacial layers can be prevented (Figure 6e).[101] ${ \mathrm { A l s o } } ,$ the metallic s-orbitals of oxide semiconductors can increase the stability of the channel material by limiting the structural disorder and charge-trapping effects.[102–104] Ferroelectric transistors with $\mathrm { I n Z n O } _ { x }$ channel layers have also been reported (Figure $6 \mathrm { f } ) . ^ { [ 2 3 ] }$ A lower operation voltage $( 5 \mathrm { V } )$ and outstanding endurance characteristics $( 1 0 ^ { 8 }$ cycles) compared to those of conventional charge-trap flash memories have been observed using oxide semiconductor channels, indicating the high potential of ferroelectric transistors as next-generation memory devices (Figure 6g,h). Moreover, this approach can be directly implemented in 3D NAND structures (Figure 6i).[23,61,105] The replacement of the charge-trapping mechanism with ferroelectric polarization leads a several-hundred-times higher

operation speed and low operation voltage. Additionally, owing to the importance of the on-current of the channel with numerous gate stacks, the high mobility of oxide semiconductor channels promotes 3D NAND applications. Although superior endurance characteristics can be achieved using oxide semiconductor channels, oxide-semiconductor-based ferroelectric transistors are usually demonstrated using bottom-gate structures. The feasibility of oxide-semiconductor-based ferroelectric transistors such as the top-gate and gate-all-around structure should be further demonstrated. Also, the investigation on the high-performance p-type oxide semiconductors can facilitate the development of ferroelectric transistor arrays and circuits.

# 3.4. Ferroelectric NAND

The operation of conventional NAND flash memory based on charge-trap transistors depends on the trapping and detrapping of electrons into the charge-trapping layer, such as Si3N4, and the floating gate. The threshold voltage of a chargetrap transistor increases when electrons are trapped into the charge-trapping layer; this is labeled as the programmed state. Contrarily, de-trapping of electrons from the charge-trapping layer decreases the threshold voltage; this is labeled as the erased state. As the charge-trapping mechanism incorporates the tunneling of charges through the tunneling oxide layer, the program/erase pulses should exhibit high amplitudes and long widths.[23,61,105] Additionally, as repeated tunneling through the tunneling oxide layer degrades the insulating properties of the layer, conventional NAND flash memories require a high operation voltage (>10  V) and show limited endurance properties (<106 ). Thus, a highly reliable nonvolatile memory with low power consumption is required for next-generation NAND flash memories.[105] Ferroelectric-hafnia-based transistors have been suggested as next-generation nonvolatile memories, and their array structures (such as ferroelectric NAND) have been investigated. Ferroelectric transistor memory cells can be operated at low voltages (<5 V) and high speed (<1 µs), exhibiting high endurance (over 108  cycles) along with a reasonable memory window and data retention properties (Table 1). Thus, they are promising candidates for next-generation NAND flash memory technology.[23] Additionally, ferroelectric NAND can be scaled down below the 10  nm node, whereas scaling down the conventional NAND flash memory below 20 nm is difficult because of capacitive coupling. The parasitic capacitance effect in NAND flash increases upon scaling down the device size, causing multilevel-operation issues.[106] Ferroelectric NAND based on hafnia-based ferroelectrics and oxide semiconductor channels have been fabricated for next-generation memory devices (Figure 7a).[23] The properties of oxide-semiconductorbased ferroelectric memory cells exhibit promising features, such as low operation voltage and high endurance. From the array perspective, ferroelectric NAND flash memory arrays exhibit the program, program-inhibit, and string level operations (Figure 7b,c).[107]

Currently, 3D NAND flash memory utilizes bit-cost scalable or terabit cell array transistors technology.[105] In contrast to 2D NAND flash memories, single-crystalline Si channels cannot be used in 3D structures because single-crystalline Si channels

in 3D structures are difficult to form using the conventional chemical vapor deposition method. Thus, poly-Si channels are typically used for 3D NAND flash memories; however, the low on-current (due to their lower mobility than that of crystalline Si) can limit the number of word-line stacks.[105,124,125] 3D ferroelectric NAND with oxide semiconductor channels are suitable alternatives that improve the 3D NAND cell density by overcoming the limitations of poly-Si channels in conventional 3D NAND.[23,26,101] 3D vertical ferroelectric transistors have been used to estimate the feasibility of 3D ferroelectric NAND flash memories. A vertical ferroelectric transistor array with a highly scaled gate length of 10 nm has been fabricated (Figure 7d).[26] The vertical ferroelectric transistor shows transfer characteristics with a memory window of 2  V (Figure  7e). This device, which has a gate length and channel width of 10 and 500 nm, respectively, exhibits an effective channel area of 0.005  µm2 ; this effective channel area is smaller than that shown by current 3D NAND flash memories.[26,105] Additionally, 3D ferroelectric NAND with trench-based architecture has been reported (Figure  7f).[62] Because ferroelectric transistors do not require electric field enhancement (which occurs in gate-all-around type transistors) and exhibit a low operation voltage, they can be used in trench-based 3D structures with high memory density. Recent reports indicate that 3D ferroelectric NAND devices exhibit a memory window of 2–3 V with multilevel cell characteristics. To analyze the feasibility of 3D ferroelectric NAND as a replacement for the current 3D NAND flash memory technology, the multilevel characteristics of 3D ferroelectric NAND should be further characterized. Additionally, because the quadruple-level cell (QLC) technology is currently used in 3D NAND flash memories, an accurate program method, such as the incremental step pulse program (ISPP), should also be developed for 3D ferroelectric NAND. Furthermore, the memory window of memory cells in 3D ferroelectric NAND should be increased to achieve stable multilevel characteristics.

# 3.5. Ternary Content Addressable Memories

Ternary content addressable memory (TCAM) is a type of circuit which searches the location of stored data that match the input data.[126–129] TCAMs enable rapid searches using parallel search operations; thus, they are used for applications that require high-speed searching. A TCAM cell stores three different data values (1, 0, and X), where X represents the do not care value, which indicates that it does not care whether the data value is 1 or 0.[127] Previously, TCAMs have utilized CMOS circuits with 16  transistors;[130] this system requires multiple transistors and standby power consumption. Recently, highperformance TCAMs based on emergent memory technologies, such as, phase-change memory, resistive switching memory, and ferroelectric transistors, have been proposed.[126,127,130–132] Among the diverse memory types, ferroelectric transistors are promising candidates owing to their CMOS compatibility, low leakage current, and high operation speed.[133–135] Ferroelectric TCAMs have been fabricated by integrating two ferroelectric transistors (Figure 8a,b)[134] with the same match-line; each gate electrode is connected to separate search-lines (Figure 8c). The match-line potential remains high when the stored data

are identical to the input search data because no discharge current flows through the match-line. However, when the input search data do not match the stored data in the TCAM cell, a large discharge current flows through the match-line. Ferroelectric-transistor-based TCAMs, which are produced by integrating two ferroelectric transistors, exhibit extremely small device area and are expected to exhibit a superior performance to that of TCAM cells based on other emergent memories (such as phase-change memory and resistive switching memory). However, these transistors are in their early stage of development, and further experimentation on high-density ferroelectric TCAM arrays and their properties is required.

# 4. Ferroelectric Transistors for Neuromorphic Applications

# 4.1. Ferroelectric Transistors for Artificial Synapses and Neurons

Neuromorphic computing has recently been reported as a nextgeneration computing method that can increase computational speed for tasks such as image recognition.[136–138] To produce neuromorphic computing hardware, developing an artificial synapse that can mimic the functions of the biological synapse is vital.[139] In neural networks (NNs), the artificial synapse emulates the weight-update characteristics of the biological synapse, and the conductance of the artificial synapse is used as

the synaptic weight. The potentiation/depression characteristics of an artificial synapse are crucial; they indicate the ability to strengthen or weaken the conductance of the device.[140] Potentiation/depression characteristics influence the multilevel characteristics of memory devices; a highly linear and symmetric conductance change with small device-to-device and cycle-to-cycle variations are essential in ensuring the acceptable performance of a NN based on artificial synapses.[141,142] Additionally, the synaptic properties of artificial synapses (such as dynamic range, retention, and endurance) influence the accuracy of NN. The dynamic range (the ratio of the maximum and minimum conductance of the artificial synapse) is vital for a high recognition accuracy. Also, the artificial synapse should exhibit long data retention and high cycle endurance to ensure the reliability of the NN. Two-terminal devices (such as, phasechange memories, spintronic devices, and resistive-switching memories) have been proposed as next-generation memory and synaptic devices.[143–145] These devices can be integrated into area-efficient array structures with a feature size of 4F2. However, when these devices are implemented in crossbar arrays, they exhibit several issues (including cross-talk, sneak path currents, and nonlinear current–voltage characteristics), and the current flowing through unselected devices can affect the readoperation accuracy. In contrary, in arrays composed of threeterminal devices, the read operation occurs without a sneak path current because the program and read operations are not affected by the states of the unselected devices. Therefore,

ferroelectric transistors based on hafnia-based ferroelectrics exhibit several advantageous properties, such as CMOS compatibility, high operation speed, low operation voltage, and high scalability. Ferroelectric materials show spontaneous polarization characteristics, which can be precisely controlled by optimizing the external electric field. Because the polarization states affect channel conductance in ferroelectric transistors, these transistors have been suggested as artificial synapses. The first-reported ferroelectric-transistor-based artificial synapse utilizes Si-doped ferroelectric hafnia (Figure 9a).[146] The artificial synapse exhibits linear and symmetric potentiation/depression characteristics under optimized pulse scheme (Figure 9b). Several other ferroelectric transistors have been subsequently proposed as analog synapses.[99,100,107,121,120] The NN based on a ferroelectric transistor with a finFET structure is expected to show high recognition accuracy (96.3%) for handwritten images by optimizing the algorithm and architecture of NNs.[147] Because the conductance values of artificial synapses require frequent input-data-based tuning, the device should exhibit high endurance properties. Thus, oxide semiconductor channels facilitate artificial synapse applications.[100,107,121] Ferroelectric transistors with oxide semiconductor (InGaZnO ) channels have been proposed as high-performance artificial synapses. They exhibit highly linear and symmetric potentiation/depres-

sion characteristics under incremental voltages with increasing pulse amplitudes (Figure  9c). Because interfacial-layer formation is suppressed by oxide semiconductor channels, highly reliable and stable switching is observed for 100  potentiation/ depression cycles (Figure 9d). The device shows a small cycleto-cycle variation (2.36%) for 100 potentiation/depression cycles and can be used to form a high-accuracy NN. The device characteristics influence the accuracy of NNs composed of ferroelectric transistors; the cycle-to-cycle variation of potentiation/ depression characteristics should be lower than 3% to ensure a high learning accuracy of the multilayer perceptron network.[99] Oxide semiconductor channels can lead to higher on-currents and smaller device-to-device variations, which can enhance the multilevel characteristics for high-precision NNs.[107,148] Artificial synapses based on ferroelectric transistors with oxide semiconductor channels have been fabricated using ferroelectric $\mathrm { { H f Z r O } } _ { x }$ and $\mathrm { I n Z n S n O } _ { x }$ channels (Figure  9e).[100] Similar to InGaZnOx-based ferroelectric transistors, InZnSnOx-based ferroelectric transistors show linear and symmetric potentiation/ depression characteristics (Figure  9f). The NN based on these ferroelectric transistors is expected to show a high recognition accuracy (93.1%) for handwritten images that is comparable to that of an ideal NN (94.1%). Additionally, due to the absence of interfacial layers, artificial synapses with oxide semiconductor channels show high endurance values (107   cycles), facilitating frequent conductance updates (Figure  9g). Artificial synapses based on the one transistor–one ferroelectric field-effect transistor (1T–1FeFET) and two transistor–one ferroelectric fieldeffect transistor have also been proposed.[146,149] In the 1T–1FeFET structure, the additional transistor acts as the access transistor, enabling the parallel programming of multiple devices. The performance of the NN based on a 1T–1FeFET synapse has been benchmarked; the NN exhibits a higher training accuracy (90%) with handwritten images and a higher speed than those of other artificial synaptic devices. According to recent reports, ferroelectric transistors are promising candidates for high-performance NNs. However, the use of ferroelectric transistors in an artificial synapse is associated with several limitations. First, ferroelectric transistors require a read-beforewrite process because their partial polarization characteristics are induced using incremental voltage amplitudes or widths.[99,146,121] This process can complicate the weight-update process in ferroelectric transistor arrays, as the weight of each ferroelectric transistor should be determined before the application of the weight-update voltage pulse.[140] Therefore, developing ferroelectric transistors which can be operated using identical voltage pulses is vital. Studies on the ferroelectric domain and its switching dynamics could facilitate the development of ferroelectric transistors that can be updated using identical pulses. The output-current variation in ferroelectric synapse arrays attributed to the random phase distribution of hafnia-based ferroelectrics is another critical issue.[150] Hafniabased ferroelectric materials exhibit multiple crystal structures; among them, only the orthorhombic crystal structure exhibits a ferroelectric phase, while the other crystal structures show dielectric properties. The hafnia-based ferroelectric layer contains multiple grains, with each grain representing a ferroelectric or dielectric phase. A random distribution of ferroelectric and dielectric phases in scaled devices can cause a non-uniform electric

field in the ferroelectric and channel layers, generating a variable output current. This issue can be solved by optimizing the crystallization process in the hafnia-based ferroelectric layer to increase the proportion of the ferroelectric phase or decrease the grain size. Moreover, artificial neurons are a building block for neuromorphic hardware.[151,152] In general, neurons with integrate-and-fire mechanisms, which can integrate inputs and generate an action potential after a certain threshold, are used. After action-potential generation (labeled firing), the membrane potential undergoes a reset (labeled the refractory period).[153] Previously, these functions have been emulated using large circuits with several capacitors and inverters. Ferroelectric transistors can be switched under repeated voltage pulses with low amplitudes (the amplitude of an individual voltage pulse is insufficient for the induction of polarization switching) (Figure  9h,i);[154–156] this phenomenon, called accumulative switching, is related to the ferroelectric nucleation process. The utilization of accumulative switching phenomena and precise design of voltage pulses can lead to linear weight-update characteristics even under identical pulses.[157,158] Accumulative switching characteristics can also be used to demonstrate integrate-and-fire dynamics; this has been emulated in a single ferroelectric transistor.[156] Additionally, integrate-and-fire dynamics have been reported using different pulse amplitudes, with higher pulse amplitudes causing more frequent firing (Figure 9j). An advantage of artificial neurons based on ferroelectric transistors is the direct integration of the input into the ferroelectric layer, eliminating the requirement for external circuit elements (such as capacitors). Thus, ferroelectric transistors, which offers high energy efficiency and spike frequency due to a short refractory period, exhibit considerable potential for artificial neuron applications.[159] However, ferroelectrictransistor-based artificial neurons involve three-terminal devices, making their implementation challenging in highly scaled neuromorphic arrays. Additionally, channel percolation in ferroelectric transistors, which is derived from the random distribution of switched domains, reduces the number of accessible states.[160–163] In neuromorphic applications, particularly in artificial synapses, abrupt conductance changes owing to channel percolation reduce the reliability of multilevel characteristics. To solve this issue, further research on decreasing the ferroelectric domain size for scaled ferroelectric transistors is required.

# 4.2. Ferroelectric Transistor Arrays for NNs

NNs have attracted attention as they can effectively reduce the computation time for various classification tasks.[136–138] NNs are composed of neurons layers, with each layer connected through synapses. The number of neurons and synapses in NNs has increased exponentially to improve the training accuracy of the NN model;[164] additionally, a large number of datasets is required for a high training accuracy. NNs are usually implemented using the von Neumann-based computer architecture containing processor and memory units; however, the von-Neumann bottleneck issue, which originates from data movement between the two units, reduces the energy efficiency and increases the training time.[165,166] NNs with artificial synapses

that mimic the weight-update characteristics of the biological synapse can overcome this issue; the synaptic weight is directly multiplied with the input value, transferring the output value to the next neuron layer without any data movement between the memory and processor units. Numerous studies have proposed the utilization of diverse memory devices (such as memristors, NOR, NAND, and AND flash) for NN implementation.[143,145,167–170] However, the high operation voltage and low endurance of flash memories are unfavorable for NNs, which require frequent memory-state updates. Although it has similar structure to that of flash, the ferroelectric transistors exhibit a low write latency and energy, exhibiting high potential for lowpower neuromorphic applications.[139] A ferroelectric-synapsebased NN with an optimized operation scheme, as well as column- and row-wise parallel weight-update characteristics was experimentally demonstrated (Figure 10a,b).[107] In a parallel weight-update scheme, only the selected ferroelectric transistors in the same row (C01 and C03) show weight updates, whereas unselected ferroelectric transistors (C00  and C02) retain their original state. Based on simulation results, the convolutional NNs based on ferroelectric transistors exhibit high recognition accuracy similar to that of NNs based on ideal devices, for image classification tasks (Figure  10c,d). A 3D-ferroelectric-NAND-based NN has also been simulated for the construction of energy-efficient NNs; each layer of 3D ferroelectric NAND is used for different functions.[171] Different word-line layers are assigned for the synaptic weight, activation, and error, while the weight gradient layers and input data are applied to bitlines and outputs are estimated using simulation.[171] These 3D ferroelectric NANDs (using the proposed mapping method) are expected to yield ten-times higher energy efficiency values than those of conventional tensor-core-based NNs. Owing to the advantageous characteristics (such as low power consumption, along with high switching speed and scalability) of ferroelectric-transistor-based artificial synapses, intensive research is being carried out on ferroelectric-transistor-based NNs. Although ferroelectric-transistor-based NNs are expected to show high energy efficiency and several NN array structures exhibit promising characteristics, several specific issues should be investigated from the array perspective.[172–175] First, the high capacitance between word lines can increase the energy consumption. Additionally, the interference and disturbance characteristics can cause inaccurate NN training and thus should be rigorously investigated. The investigation and optimization of NN structures for ferroelectric transistor arrays and operation methods could facilitate the development of high-performance NNs based on ferroelectric transistors.

# 5. Conclusion

Ferroelectric materials, which have field-driven switching mechanism and nonvolatile polarization characteristics, exhibit high potential for nonvolatile memories. However, the complexity of traditional perovskite-based ferroelectric materials and limitations in their CMOS-process implementation have hindered the development of high-performance ferroelectric memories. Since the recent discovery of hafnia-based ferroelectrics, which exhibit higher $E _ { \mathrm { c } }$ scalability and CMOS compatibility

than those of perovskite-based ferroelectrics, their applicability in the fabrication of high-performance ferroelectric memories has been intensively investigated. However, further research is required for the development of high-performance ferroelectric transistors based on ferroelectric hafnia. First, developing a large memory window similar to that of conventional charge-based flash memories is crucial. Currently, charge-based flash memories are reported to show reliable triple-level cell (TLC) operations with memory windows of >6  V.[176] In addition, the QLC operation using charge-based flash memories is demonstrated using a memory cell with a memory window of 10  V.[177] Because ferroelectric transistors are reported to show a memory window of <4  V, further development is required to achieve stable TLC and QLC operations (Table  1). Diverse strategies have been used to solve this issue. The E value of hafnia-based ferroelectrics is affected by the doping element; therefore, fundamental research on optimizing hafniabased ferroelectrics and the doping element can improve the memory window of ferroelectric transistors.[36,55] Additionally, device structures with a gate stack and cell design (recessed channel, gate-all-around, and fin-structure) should be developed to enhance the memory window, and the domain size of hafnia-based ferroelectrics should be optimized.[17,18,42,115] A large domain size can limit the multilevel characteristics of highly scaled transistors.[178,179] Furthermore, the grain size of hafnia-based ferroelectrics is affected by the concentration of the doping element,[180,181] and other process parameters (such as annealing temperature and film thickness).[180,182] Therefore, the ferroelectric-grain nucleation/growth process and doping

elements should be optimized. Additionally, conventional multilevel operation methods (such as ISPP) should be optimized further for ferroelectric transistor arrays. Finally, the development of optimized array structures and operation methods for ferroelectric transistor arrays and their operation characteristics should be extensively investigated. At present, ferroelectric transistor arrays are demonstrated using large technology nodes and small array sizes than those in commercialized memories, and the large-scale integration of highly scaled devices has been rarely reported.[25,52,86] Thus, their operation characteristics and reliability in large array sizes should be validated, to estimate their feasibility as next-generation nonvolatile memory devices. Additionally, the interference and disturbance of highly scaled arrays should be evaluated to develop optimized programming methods that alleviate them. Furthermore, 2D semiconductor or ferroelectric materials can be used to develop ferroelectric transistors. 2D semiconductor/ferroelectric heterostructures with clean 2D-layer surfaces exhibit atomically clean interfaces.[183–185] Additionally, because the 2D layers can be stacked in any desired order and transferred onto arbitrary substrates, 2D-based ferroelectric transistors can be fabricated into diverse structures without penalties.

Considering neuromorphic applications, the development of potentiation/depression methods that utilize identical pulses is crucial. The read-before-write scheme used in ferroelectric transistors increases their circuit complexity, latency, and energy consumption.[100,139] To overcome this limitation, an operation method that can control the partial polarization characteristics with identical voltage pulses should be

developed.[186] Controlling the displacement current can generate stable multilevel characteristics under identical voltage pulses, while additional circuits (such as a current limiter) can be used with hafnia-based ferroelectrics to control the displacement current for precisely controlling the partial polarization.[187] Furthermore, basic studies on ferroelectric domain nucleation and switching mechanisms can facilitate the development of operation methods based on identical voltage pulses. Potentiation/depression characteristics are directly related to ferroelectric-transistor multilevel characteristics; therefore, the domain size and dynamics of the transistor should be optimized. Small domain sizes should be developed for stable potentiation/depression characteristics with small device-to-device and cycle-to-cycle variations. The output current variation in ferroelectric synapse arrays due to random phase distributions should also be analyzed.[150] Because hafnia-based ferroelectric materials contain both dielectric and ferroelectric phases, the ratio and distribution of the ferroelectric phase can affect the device characteristics by generating a non-uniform electric field in scaled devices. The thickness of ferroelectric layer and doping concentration affect the phase distribution in films.[180,188] Therefore, further optimization and analysis of the crystallization mechanism and process is required for reliable multilevel characteristics in neuromorphic applications. Inserting a thin dielectric layer between the ferroelectric layers increases the number of domains inside the hafnia-based ferroelectrics.[189,190] The increase in the number of domains can lead to reliable multilevel characteristics of ferroelectric transistors. Moreover, based on the current percolation path modeling, the optimization of the grain size of the hafnia-based ferroelectric layer and dimension (i.e., width/length) of the transistor can lead to stable multilevel characteristics.[191] Finally, the structure and properties of artificial synapses and neurons should be extensively investigated with the array operations and development of new circuit architectures that can overcome the existing non-idealities in ferroelectric devices under consideration.[186]

Although ferroelectric transistors based on hafnia-based ferroelectrics exhibit several advantageous characteristics, some issues require solutions before their use in the fabrication of high-performance next-generation memory devices. Therefore, further research is required to facilitate the development of advanced ferroelectric devices. Additionally, a deep understanding of the fundamental principles and mechanisms of hafnia-based ferroelectrics can facilitate the enhancement of device-performance.
