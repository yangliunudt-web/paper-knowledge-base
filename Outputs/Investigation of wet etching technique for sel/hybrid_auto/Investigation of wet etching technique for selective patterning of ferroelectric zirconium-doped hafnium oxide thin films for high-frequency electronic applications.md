# Investigation of wet etching technique for selective patterning of ferroelectric zirconium-doped hafnium oxide thin films for high-frequency electronic applications

# A B S T R A C T

This paper presents the area-selective wet etching (ASWE) method as a novel approach to have a selective patterning of a 6.8 nm-thick zirconium-doped hafnium oxide (HZO) thin film, to improve the performance of a metal ferroelectric metal (MFM)-like structure. According to the electromagnetic simulations of microwave phase shifters with patterned HZO thin films, it is underlined the importance to have selectively targeted areas covered with HZO instead of full-coverage wafers, to gain a further increase in the microwave performance of low-voltage tunable high-frequency components. The impact of the ASWE method on the morpho-structural properties was studied using various investigation tools in a non-destructive manner. X-ray reflectivity (XRR) has been employed at different immersion times, up to 120 s. Based on the extended Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) analysis, as well as from the simulation of the experimental curves in the framework of parallel-tempering algorithm, the determination of the etching rate became possible. X-ray diffraction (XRD), atomic force microscopy (AFM), and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) clearly indicated the complete removal of HZO after etching processes at 180 s. The method is fast, reliable, and low-cost, thus filling the actual gap in providing the necessary ferroelectric thin films exclusively in selected areas of interest.

# 1. Introduction

The discovery of a stable orthorhombic ferroelectric phase in HfO2, which was induced through the doping of various materials, has created new pathways for integrating ferroelectric device concepts, such as memories, and has brought an important contribution to the downscaling of the electronic devices [1]. In recent years, extensive research has been conducted on zirconium-doped hafnium oxide (HZO or HfO:Zr) thin films [2–5] as a ferroelectric material for various applications, which include interdigitated capacitor-like phase shifters, phased antenna arrays, microwave filters, resistive switching, and non-volatile memories [6–14]. Studies have demonstrated that the optimal thickness for achieving the highest proportion of the orthorhombic phase (i.

e., the ferroelectric phase) is typically not greater than 10 nm, while the thicker films tend to be more monoclinic, for which the ferroelectricity is lost [15].

For the deposition of HZO thin films several techniques are used including atomic layer deposition (ALD) [16–20], ion beam sputtering (IBS) [21], radio-frequency (RF) co-sputtering technique [22–24], and pulsed laser deposition (PLD) [25–29]. All these techniques can be optimised based on the specific requirements of the HZO thin films, such as crystal structure, morphology, and electrical properties. Among these, ALD has several important advantages when compared to other techniques for HZO thin films deposition at thickness below 10 nm. It can deposit highly conformal and uniform films with sub-nanometric thickness control, thus allowing the deposition of ultra-thin films with

precise electrical properties. Also, it enables a high level of control over structural defect density and stoichiometry, which is essential for HZO thin films’ quality and chemical composition [30]. Additionally, the high compatibility with a wide range of substrates represents another important advantage. In contrast, other techniques may have limitations in depositing uniform and precise films, resulting in higher structural defect density and lower stoichiometric control, which could have a detrimental effect to the HZO thin films’ electrical properties. The performance of state-of-the-art HZO-based electronic devices is currently investigated using continuous HZO films deposited on the whole wafer surface and mostly resuming to a metal-ferroelectric-metal (MFM)-like structure by confining the ferroelectric film in between two titanium nitride (TiN) layers [31–36], to assure the formation of the ferroelectric phase. Moreover, when HZO is deposited on a metal surface used as bottom electrode, it can form a ferroelectric capacitor that can be used as a phase shifter. However, these films deposited over the entire metallic surface exhibit intrinsic limitations, such as unwanted losses due to leakage current and parasitic capacitance, further related to detrimental effects in device’s performance, such as decreased bandwidth and increased insertion loss [37,38]. To overcome this drawback, the scientific community has urged to develop effective methods for patterning HZO films, to further create selective areas with different properties or functionality. More specifically, a precise geometrical configuration not only offers the possibility of integrating the material into a complex front-end, but also allows the preservation/retention of the desired ferroelectric properties only in the selected areas of the final device topology [3]. Moreover, developing well-controlled etching processes that allow patterning HZO thin films only in selected areas is essential for the precise integration of ferroelectric-based devices, such as capacitors, phase shifters or ferroelectric tunnel junctions, etc., into a much more complex electronic platform for CMOS-compatible or nextgeneration high-frequency applications.

As existing etching techniques, dry etching (DE) and wet etching (WE) are widely employed in various industries and research fields for material removal and patterning. DE, also known as plasma etching, involves the use of reactive gases and plasma, which chemically reacts with the material of interest, thus resulting in its removal from the substrate. WE utilizes liquid chemical solutions to dissolve and selectively remove a material based on etchant’s reaction with the material under test. DE provides higher selectivity, faster etching rates, and better control over feature shapes, but it can induce surface damage and requires more complex equipment. On the contrary, WE is a simpler and more cost-effective method with milder chemical reactions, but it may have lower selectivity, slower etching rates, and limited control over feature shapes [39]. The choice between DE and WE depends on the specific material, the requirements of the fabrication process, and the desired outcome. Unfortunately, today’s approaches for selective patterned areas of HZO are very scarce in the literature. Recently, Mauersberger et al. [40] presented a single-step reactive ion etching (RIE) process for device integration of HZO/TiN stacks, that can simultaneously etch both materials and, thus, when a TiN layer is required in the final device, this approach is not suitable. In a different study, Hoffman et al. showed that the atomic layer etching (ALE) of ferroelectric HZO thin films enables giant tunnelling electroresistance [41]. ALE and RIE are well-established techniques in semiconductor manufacturing and offer unique advantages in specific scenarios. They are compatible with established fabrication equipment and workflows, ensuring efficient production. Both methods can be seamlessly integrated into the semiconductor manufacturing process, allowing for multi-step fabrication sequences and compatibility with other deposition and lithography steps. This integration enables the creation of highly sophisticated semiconductor devices with precise control over the etching process. While ALE and RIE offer certain advantages in the field of selective patterning, there are also several disadvantages when applying these techniques to 6.8 nm-thick HZO thin films. ALE and RIE processes introduce complexity and higher costs due to the need for

specialized equipment and precise process control [42]. These techniques can also cause damage and degradation to the delicate thin films, thus compromising their electrical properties and, hence, the performance of the final device [43]. Achieving high selectivity with ALE and RIE can be challenging, leading to potential etching issues and reduced control over the etching depth [42]. Moreover, anisotropic etching behaviour in ALE and RIE may result in uneven sidewall profiles and unwanted artifacts [44–46]. Additionally, process integration complexities and equipment constraints can limit scalability and large-scale production. Considering the need for a thorough evaluation of these drawbacks in conjunction with the specific requirements, the wet etching method emerges as a more favourable candidate for the selective patterning of ferroelectric 6.8 nm-thick HZO thin films. It should be noted that the WE process can reduce the surface destruction, which may be produced during the DE process, because the etchant molecules react only with the surface atoms [47]. Also, it can be used to uniformly etch the surface of a large area [48]. Another major advantage of WE is the ability to be customized according to the material to be etched, which implies that this technique benefits from high selectivity for different materials [49]. The special features of WE encouraged us to try the WE for patterning of HZO films. The presented outcomes can have major implications in the design of micro- and nano-electronic systems, since they allow to optimise the integration of materials science with high-frequency engineering applications, for which it is essential to understand the effects of materials’ properties on the electrical characteristics of the circuit under test. For this reason, it is of utmost importance to avoid any undesired interference among neighbouring components, and this can be realised by a careful design aimed at confining materials’ properties just in specific regions.

We report here the selective patterned areas of ferroelectric 6.8 nm HZO film by WE, thus filling the actual gap in providing the necessary ferroelectric thin films mostly in the selected area of interest, without affecting the surrounding circuitry, as proven by AFM images. The optimisation of the etch rate was achieved in a non-destructive manner by analysing of the Kiessig interference fringes of the X-ray reflectivity spectra. Our findings clearly demonstrate the benefits given by the areaselective wet etching (ASWE) on the capacitance value in the range of 5–10 V of the MFM structure. Furthermore, according to the electro magnetic design and simulations of microwave phase shifters with patterned HZO thin films, the microwave performance of low-voltage tunable high-frequency components is enhanced. Thus, our fast, simple, reliable, and low-cost method could be used for further developments in a broad range of neuromorphic and electronic 5G and 6G applications.

# 2. Experimental

# 2.1. Materials and reagents

Hydrofluoric acid (HF) and acetone were purchased from Sigma Aldrich. The n-type 〈100〉 Si wafers with thickness of 525 µm and resistivity of 10 Ω⋅cm have been used as complementary metal-–oxide–semiconductor (CMOS)-compatible substrates that provide welldeveloped lithography processes. The positive photoresists were purchased from Microchemicals, Ulm, Germany and then have been used in the wet etching processes. The precursors used for the ALD process are (i) Tetrakis(dimethylamino)hafnium (TDMAH, 99.99+%-Hf, <0.2%-Zr, Puratrem, USA) for hafnium, and Tetrakis(dimethylamino)zirconium (IV) (TDMAZ, 99.99%-Zr, Puratrem, USA) for zirconium. Ultra-pure water has been used as oxidizing agent, and ultra-high purity nitrogen (Nitrogen 6.0, 99.9999 vol%) has been deployed for purging and carrier gas.

# 2.2. Methods and equipment

The structural features of the investigated thin films (grown by ALD

using an OpAl ALD reactor - Oxford Instruments Plasma Technology) have been assessed using a 9 kW SmartLab diffractometer (Osaka, Japan), operated at 40 kV with 75 mA, that provides an incident X-ray wavelength of 0.1504 nm. During the grazing incidence X-ray diffraction (GI-XRD) measurement, the incidence angle has been kept at $0 . 3 5 ^ { \circ }$ , while the detector angle has been varied from 20 to 70◦. A mediumresolution setup has been used with narrow slits at detector’s side to reduce the instrumental broadening. The diffraction peaks indexing has been made with ICDD (International Center for Diffraction Data) database. X-ray reflectivity (XRR) investigations have been performed in θ/2θ mode in the 0–10◦ range.

The surface topography of the sensing films has been investigated by scanning electron microscopy (SEM), using a field emission gun scanning electron microscope/FEG-SEM-Nova NanoSEM 630 (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) (FEI), with superior low voltage resolution and high surface sensitivity imaging.

X-ray photoemission spectroscopy (XPS) spectra have been acquired using a Sigma Surface Science photoelectron spectrometer. The equipment is designed with a 160-mm hemispherical energy analyser, a 1D detector (ASPECT), and an Al Kα X-ray source operated at 13 kV and 200 W. The analysed sample had an area of $2 \times 2 ~ \mathrm { m m } ^ { 2 }$ . The chamber’s pressure was maintained at a value below $3 \times { 1 0 } ^ { - 9 }$ mbar.

The RF magnetron sputtering deposition was realised with the Oxford Instruments-PlasmaLab System 100, with a 6-inch diameter titanium high purity target (Testbourne Ltd).

Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM) characterizations were performed in Intermittent-Contact mode with a NtegraScanning Probe Microscope (NT_MDT Spectrum Instruments, United States).

The RF magnetron sputtering depositions have been performed using argon (Ar) and molecular nitrogen (N) as sputtering and reactive gases respectively, at an RF power of 300 W. The flow rate of the respective gases has been set to 30 sccm for Ar and 1 sccm for N, individually monitored by 1179A mass flow controllers using a closed loop system. A base pressure of 5 mTorr has been reached by an Alcatel ATH500M (Pfeiffer Vacuum, Aßlar, Germany) turbo pump backed by an Alcatel ACP40G dry pump and monitored by an active Penning gauge. The

substrate holder has been cooled with water to keep the substrate temperature at room value. The substrate oscillates under the target by a 30◦ angle.

# 2.3. The ALD of thin film based on HZO

The ferroelectric HZO thin films have been developed by doping of $\mathrm { H f O } _ { 2 }$ with Zr. The $\mathrm { H f O _ { 2 } }$ layer doped with Zr has been deposited by ALD (using an OpAl ALD reactor - Oxford Instruments Plasma Technology) with the method of sequential mixing on the surface, in a variable proportion, of the two precursors (Hf and Zr) followed by the oxidation step. This method conducts to obtain the binary oxide thin films, homogeneous in volume, with a low content of Zr. The growth rate of the zirconium doped $\mathrm { H f O _ { 2 } }$ layer was ~1.09 Å/ALD cycle at 200 ◦C [9]. Prior to each ALD process, the silicon wafers, coated with TiN, were subjected to a UV-ozone cleaning-activation treatment for 15 min at $7 5 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ using a UV-ozone cleaner (Novascan Technologies, USA).

# 2.5. Wet etching process

It is widely known that HF acid is able to etch several oxides, such as silicon dioxide (SiO2) or hafnium oxide (HfO2). For the wet etching of HZO thin films, a diluted HF solution has been used. The solution has been first tested at a concentration of 20% and the action has proven to be so violent that the whole HZO film has been over-etched, also under the PR, meaning that the strongly isotropic etching occurs at a high rate. This indicates that the etching process was not only rapid but also aggressive. Such violent etching leads to poor control over the etching process, which is crucial for nanoscale HZO thin films. Therefore, to ensure precise control over the etching process, a lower concentration of 1% HF etchant was made by diluting the solution in water. This dilution helps to moderate the etchant’s reactivity and improving the precision and uniformity of the etching process. By utilizing a low concentration of 1% HF etchant, the desired results can be achieved without damaging the HZO film or losing control over the etching process. This solution has been prepared in large amount (2 L) and kept in the chemical storage room for several months. In the process of WE, the environmental conditions play a crucial role in determining the success of the etching process. # 3. Results and discussion

# 3.1. Optimisation of the wet etching method

To quantify the effects of the wet etching process in a non-destructive manner, XRR investigations have been performed at different stages of etching. Fig. 2A presents the XRR patterns (black lines) for the reference unetched HZO, as well as after different immersion time value t (i.e., from 10 s to 120 s).

One can observe that each XRR pattern exhibits a couple of interference fringes, whose period is modified as the immersion time increases. These interference fringes are known as Kiessig interference fringes and stem from the multiple interference of the X-rays at the boundary between layer and substrate [51]. The layer thickness τ is related to the interference fringes period Δθ as follows: $\tau = \lambda / \Delta \theta ,$ , where λ is the incident X-ray wavelength. The experimental curves were simulated considering the $\mathrm { H f O } _ { 2 } / \mathrm { S i O } _ { 2 } / \mathrm { S i }$ stacking with density, thickness, and roughness for up layer and intermediate one as free parameter. In the case of Si substrate, the density was fixed at $2 . 3 2 g / \mathrm { c m } ^ { 3 }$ , while the thickness is an undefined variable. The simulated curves are shown in Fig. 2A with red line, for which the goodness of the fits is confirmed by the smaller values of the R and $\chi ^ { 2 }$ fit parameters $( \mathrm { i . e . } ,$ , below 0.06). Additionally, the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) method has been employed to determine the layer thickness, considering the periodicity of interference fringes as can be found in Fig. 2B. The graphical representation between the FFT intensity and the thickness gives two features: one of them is located at 1.8 nm and becomes smoother and smoother as the immersion time increases while the second one is located at higher thickness, but its position is shifted towards left as the immersion time increases. If the first feature could be ascribed to a thin native $S \mathrm { i O } _ { 2 } ,$ the last one is attributed to the HZO film. Accordingly, the layer thickness varies from 6.8 nm for the unetched film down to 2.7 nm when the immersion time is 120 s. The corresponding thickness in each case can be found in Fig. 2B.

In order to confirm the XRR investigations, grazing-incidence XRD was employed. The XRD spectra before and after the etching process at an immersion time of 180 s are illustrated in Fig. 2D. Prior to the etching process, the XRD spectrum displays a narrow diffraction peak attributed to the Si substrate, along with two broad diffraction features centred at approximately 32˚and 55˚, which can be ascribed to (111) and (22 0) of orthorhombic $\mathrm { H f O _ { 2 } }$ (card no. 040-1173) with $\mathbf { a } = 0 . 3 3 \ \mathrm { n m }$ , b = 0.55 nm, and $\mathtt { c } = 0 . 6 4 \ \mathrm { n m }$ . Subsequent XPS investigations will reveal that the concentration of Zr is insufficient for Xray detection, as the small crystal size falls below the technique’s resolution limit. The small intensity, as well as the large broadening of the diffraction peak are attributed to the small thickness of the ferroelectric layer $( \mathrm { e } . g . , < 7$ nm). According to Scherrer’s equation, the size of the crystalline domains is around 10 nm, which indicates a weak crystallization at the actual thickness. In this case, the peak position becomes dependent on the azimuthal angle (ϕ) of the sample in relation to the incident beam. # 3.2. Wet etching method validation

Extensive research was conducted on the etching procedure of HZO to demonstrate its potential for selectively patterning specific areas uniformly across the surface. This technique is crucial to enhance the performance of high-frequency or low-voltage electronic structures. For the experimental validation of the proposed WE technique for ultra-thin HZO layers, we fabricated and measured metal-ferroelectric-meal (MFM) structures on doped Si, using TiN as top and bottom electrodes. This configuration was chosen to allow an easy assessment of the nanoscale devices.

The MFM structure was designed in CleWin software and is illustrated in Fig. 5A. The fabrication process was identical to the one shown in Fig. 1, until the deposition of HZO was accomplished. Subsequently, a separate set of photolithographic masks was used. The initial mask was created for the purpose of etching the HZO layer in undesired regions, specifically the areas between the structures. The wet etching technique, carried out under the conditions described above, was employed to generate squares of variable sizes: 450 µm × 450 µm, 400 µm × 400 µm, and 350 µm × 350 µm (shown in Fig. 5A upper side). The second photolithographic process was then employed to pattern the top electrode’s TiN layer (90 nm) by lift-off. Following the HZO etching procedure, the surface of the wafer was analysed using SEM. Fig. 5B provides a top view of the wafer, which displays both etched and unetched areas of HZO. The unetched regions representing the patterned HZO areas were observed in the SEM image from Fig. 5A (lower side) in three different square geometries with calculated dimensions: 445 µm × 445 µm, 390 µm × 390 µm, and 338 µm × 338 µm, respectively. Upon comparing these dimensions to the theoretical ones, a standard deviation lower than 5% was observed, proving that the proposed wet etching method is a reliable method for the configuration of thin layers of HZO, thus confirming the effectiveness of the WE technique in producing high-quality selectively patterned HZO thin film areas. The TiN top electrode was patterned into smaller squares than the HZO squares. The final MFM structure was examined using SEM, and the top-view of the structures was also highlighted in Fig. 5B. A cross-sectional SEM image (Fig. 5C) was also captured to observe the MFM structure, which consisted of a 6.8 nm HZO layer sandwiched between TiN bottom and top electrodes.

The capacitance–voltage (C-V) electrical characterization was performed on-wafer by means of a Keithley SCS 4200 station. One can observe that the WE process preserves the capacitance value up to 5 V, whereas the latter presents twofold higher values, in the range 5–10 V,

than the unpatterned HZO layer, reaching almost 9 nF (Fig. 5D). These results suggest two main outcomes for the proposed WE technique: (1) it does not hinder the ferroelectric (orthorhombic) phase of the HZO thin film; (2) it allows improving the performance of the MFM-like structure in terms of its maximum capacitance, which could be beneficial for microwave applications in which a varactor with high-tunability characteristics is necessary to design high-performance phase shifters (e.g., in filters or phased antenna arrays). It is possible that the observed increase in the capacitance value after the etching process is related to the fact that the HZO films deposited over the entire metallic surface exhibit intrinsic limitations, such as unwanted losses due to leakage currents and parasitic capacitances coming from interface effects. When the HZO is selectively patterned only under the top TiN electrode, the losses are limited, thus leading to an increase in the capacitance of the device. In addition, the selective patterning of the HZO film could result in a more uniform and controlled ferroelectric polarisation phenomenon, which could contribute to the observed increase in the capacitance. This is because the selective patterning of the HZO film ensures that the ferroelectric polarisation occurs more effectively only in the desired regions. Besides this, it is likely that local fringing effects (not easy to quantify analytically and/or experimentally) take place at the edges of the top electrode of the MFM capacitor, and this is a well-known cause for the increase of the capacitance. If we look more in detail at the C-V curves in the voltage range between − 2 V and 2 V (Fig. 6A), we can observe the typical hysteretic behaviour in both the unpatterned and patterned cases, but with a pronounced asymmetry for the unpatterned HZO (a shift of the minimum towards − 1 V is evident). When patterning the thin HZO layer with the proposed ASWE technique, a symmetrisation occurs with an upward shift of the minimum towards − 0.5 V. These results are also confirmed by the polarisation-electric field (P-E) hysteresis loops shown in Fig. 6B (and obtained with the Sawyer-Tower circuit method): the symmetrisation effect is apparent for the patterned HZO, with excellent values for the coercive electric field $E _ { c }$ ≈ 1.4 MV/cm, the saturation polarisation of about $7 8 ~  { \mu \mathrm { C } } /  { \mathrm { c m } } ^ { 2 } ;$ , and the remanent polarisation $P _ { r } \approx 2 5 \ : \mu \mathrm { C } / \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 }$ . Hence, we can conclude that the ASWE technique does not hinder the ferroelectric properties of the HZO layer at all, meaning that the proposed method can be effective in providing more controllable electrical properties of ultra-thin ferroelectric layers.

In order to assess the further potential applications of the presented WE technique, in the following, we will show the design and simulation of microwave phase shifters based on selectively deposited HZO and their ability to work at a DC bias voltage of just a few volts (i.e., between − 3 and 0.3 V).

# 5. Conclusions

In this paper, we have reported the area-selective wet etching as a promising approach to obtain the selective patterning of HZO thin film. Since the etching of a thin film with a thickness below 10 nm is challenging to observe by microscopic techniques, X-ray reflectivity was extensively used to determine the optimal concentration of etchant solutions, as well as to get the etching rate and, further, to establish the most suitable etching time for a 6.8 nm HZO film. In addition, other investigations tools, such as XRD, SEM, AFM, and XPS techniques indicate the complete removal of HZO after the proposed etching method. We provided clear experimental evidence that the ASWE process improves the performance of the MFM-like structure in terms of its maximum capacitance, which could be beneficial for microwave applications. In this respect, we designed HZO-based phase shifters working in the upper part of the microwave spectrum and based on unpatterned and patterned HZO: the electromagnetic simulations demonstrate that a great benefit can be achieved from the patterning of the thin HZO layer, as this solution maximises the ferroelectric effect only in the areas of
