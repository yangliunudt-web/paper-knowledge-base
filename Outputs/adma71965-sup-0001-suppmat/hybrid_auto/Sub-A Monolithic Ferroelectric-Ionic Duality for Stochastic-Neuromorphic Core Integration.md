Hafnium-zirconium oxide (HZO) has emerged as a leading ferroelectric material for nanoscale electronic applications due to its unique combination of scalability, complementary metal-oxidesemiconductor (CMOS) compatibility, and robust polarization behavior. The ability to modulate the Hf/Zr ratio offers a wide tunable range of ferroelectric and antiferroelectric phase stability, enabling precise control over electrical characteristics. HZO maintains strong polarization switching even at sub-nm thickness, supporting aggressive device scaling without sacrificing functional performance. [1–4] In addition, HZO crystallizes at relatively low temperatures, allowing integration with backend-of-line (BEOL) processes and minimizing thermal budget concerns during fabrication. Its compatibility with atomic layer deposition (ALD) processes enables conformal growth with excellent thickness and composition control across complex architectures.

Importantly, these advantages stand in sharp contrast to conventional perovskite ferroelectrics such as PZT or SBT, which typically require high crystallization temperatures, thick films, and non-CMOS materials. Such requirements hinder monolithic integration with advanced silicon technologies. By comparison, HZO exhibits robust ferroelectricity in several-nanometer films using CMOS-compliant low-temperature processes, making it inherently suited for modern semiconductor manufacturing.

These attributes—combined with its demonstrated endurance, low-power switching capability, and integration maturity—make HZO a highly suitable platform for implementing ferroelectric functionalities in advanced logic, memory, and neuromorphic computing systems. Furthermore, these intrinsic material advantages are directly linked to the CMOS compatibility of the FIDFET structure. The low crystallization temperature and ALD-based conformality of HZO allows its direct integration into existing high-k metal-gate (HKMG) process flows without additional high-temperature annealing or non-standard materials, ensuring full BEOL compliance.

To evaluate the crystallographic phases present in the HZO thin films, grazing-incidence X-ray diffraction (GIXRD) measurements were performed using a high-resolution diffractometer (X’pert Pro) with a fixed incident angle of $0 . 5 ^ { \circ } { } _ { ; }$ , optimizing surface sensitivity and accuracy for thin film analysis. A prominent diffraction signal appears near 30.5°, which includes overlapping contributions from the orthorhombic (111)o and tetragonal (011)t planes. The orthorhombic phase is well-known for its ferroelectric properties, while the tetragonal phase is generally anti-ferroelectric properties. Because these peaks are closely spaced, the precise separation of their contributions is nontrivial. To resolve this ambiguity, peak deconvolution was carried out assuming that the (111)o and (011)t reflections are centered at 30.4°, and 30.8°, respectively. [5–7] A distinct peak at $3 1 . 6 ^ { \circ }$ is attributed to the monoclinic $( 1 1 1 ) _ { \mathrm { m } }$ phase, which is also non-ferroelectric. The relative proportions of the monoclinic, tetragonal, and orthorhombic phases were extracted by integrating the area under each deconvoluted peak.

To further verify the crystallographic nature of the HZO grains at the nanoscale, fast Fourier transform (FFT) analysis was performed using high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (Cscorrected TEM). This technique enables direct determination of interplanar spacings, supporting phase identification at the individual grain. The inset of the image presents the FFT patterns highlighting the measured d-spacings across representative grains. The results from both GIXRD and FFT analyses consistently confirm the dominance of the orthorhombic phase, validating its key role in enabling ferroelectric behavior in the fabricated HZO films.

Note S3: Previously reported roles of oxygen vacancies in ferroelectric HfO2 films

Oxygen vacancies $\mathrm { ( V _ { O } s ) }$ are one of the most frequently observed intrinsic defects in HfO2-based thin films and play a critical role in determining the physical, electrical, and structural characteristics of ferroelectric devices. Excessive or uncontrolled $\mathrm { V } _ { \mathrm { O } }$ presence is generally regarded as detrimental in the following aspects[8–11]:

1. Unwanted trap formation and leakage current

VO creates localized states within the bandgap, acting as electron trap centers. These traps enhance leakage current through Poole-Frenkel emission and trap-assisted tunneling, ultimately degrading the insulating performance of the ferroelectric layer.

2. Suppressed switching and wake-up degradation

In ultra-thin ferroelectric layers, even a small number of VO can locally pin ferroelectric dipoles, suppressing polarization reversal and leading to memory window shrinkage in ferroelectric applications. As cycling progresses, these pinned dipoles are gradually released due to $\mathrm { V _ { O } }$ distribution, leading to an increase in remnant polarization—a phenomenon known as the wake-up effect.

3. Crystallographic instability

While moderate $\mathrm { V _ { O } }$ concentration may help stabilize the polar orthorhombic phase, high VO concentration destabilizes it in favor of non-ferroelectric tetragonal or monoclinic phases. This destabilization impairs switching ability and ferroelectric reliability.

4. Interfacial degradation

At interfaces with oxygen-scavenging electrodes (e.g., TiN), the accumulation of $\mathrm { V _ { O S } }$ can promote the formation of non-ferroelectric interlayers $( \mathrm { e . g . , T i O _ { x } N _ { y } ) }$ . These interfacial layers degrade polarization retention and reduce endurance by introducing defect-rich, non-switchable regions near the electrode interface.

To analyze the oxygen distribution across the device stack, two-dimensional spectrum images were acquired using scanning transmission electron microscopy coupled with electron energy loss spectroscopy (STEM-EELS), as shown in Figure 1D; Figures S4 and S5. The energy dispersion was set to 0.25 eV per channel. Spectra were collected across both the low-loss and core-loss regions, spanning from -29.85 eV to 256.95 eV and from 370.15 eV to 656.95 $\mathrm { e V , }$ respectively. Quantitative analysis of oxygen content was performed using Gatan Microscopy Suite (GMS 3), adopting a methodology adapted from the approach by Verbeeck and Van $\operatorname { s e r t } .$ [12] The oxygen signal was extracted from the K-edge, and background subtraction was carried out using a power-law fit. Hartree-Slater cross-section modeling was employed for edge fitting, excluding the first 40.0 eV of the ELNES (Energy Loss Near Edge Structure) to ensure improved fitting accuracy over the 500.3- 657.0 eV ranges. The resulting oxygen concentration maps offer a spatially resolved comparison of oxygen content within different regions of the transistors. In addition to quantification, the fine structure of the O K-edge ELNES was used to prove local electronic states influenced by $\mathrm { V _ { O } s . }$ . The O K-edge spectrum exhibits two characteristic peaks: $p _ { 1 }$ (~534.9 eV) and $p _ { 2 }$ (~538.38 eV), corresponding to transitions from O 1s core electrons to unoccupied conduction band states. The observed peaks predominantly stem from O 1s electron excitations into unoccupied states that are hybridized with Hf/Zr d orbitals. Their relative intensities are influenced by the local structural and chemical surroundings, such as crystallinity and $\mathrm { V } _ { \mathrm { O } }$ concentration. Notably, prior core-hole-included EELS simulations of various $\mathrm { H f O } _ { 2 }$ polymorphs have demonstrated that the $p _ { 1 }$ peak tends to diminish in oxygen-deficient environments. [13] In this work, we utilize the $p _ { 1 } / p _ { 2 }$ intensity ratio in O K-edge as a spatial probe for identifying variations in $\mathrm { V } _ { \mathrm { O } }$ distribution across the HZO films.

Note S6: TiO2 interlayer selection

The $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ interlayer was selected as an interfacial engineering layer for the FIDFET because it uniquely satisfies the electrical, chemical, and process requirements needed to realize hybrid ferroelectric-ionic operation.

(1) High-k dielectric constant enabling efficient field coupling

$\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ possesses a high dielectric constant, which minimizes voltage drop across the interlayer and ensures efficient electric field coupling into the HZO film. In contrast to low-k dielectrics such as $\mathrm { { A l } } _ { 2 } \mathrm { { O } } _ { 3 } .$ —where substantial voltage drop occurs even for ultrathin films— $- \mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ does not hinder ferroelectric switching, even when inserted as a 0.5 nm layer.

(2) Strong oxygen affinity enabling controlled $\mathrm { V _ { O } }$ generation

Ti-based materials are intrinsically oxygen-deficient and exhibit low formation energy for $\mathrm { V _ { O } s . }$ . This high oxygen affinity allows $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ or Ti to act as an oxygen scavenger at the HZO interface, withdrawing $O ^ { 2 - }$ ions from the adjacent HZO lattice and thereby increasing interfacial $\mathrm { V } _ { \mathrm { O } }$ concentration.[15-21] This property enables controllable modulation of interfacial $\mathrm { V _ { O } }$ concentration without causing structural degradation, which is essential for achieving the ferroelectric-ionic coupling and RTN-based stochastic behavior in the FIDFET.

By contrast, alternative high-k dielectrics such as $\mathrm { H f O } _ { 2 }$ or $\mathrm { Z r O } _ { 2 }$ —though chemically compatible— exhibit similar oxygen chemical potentials to HZO, resulting in negligible interfacial $\mathrm { V } _ { \mathrm { O } }$ modulation. This makes them unsuitable for inducing ionic activity while maintaining ferroelectric stability. Overall, $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ provides an optimal combination of high-k electrostatics, moderate oxygen reactivity, and full CMOS compatibility, enabling controlled $\mathrm { V } _ { \mathrm { O } }$ engineering essential for the FIDFET’s ferroelectric-ionic duality function.

Note S7: Thickness optimization of $\mathrm { T i O _ { 2 } }$

To evaluate the thickness-dependent role of $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 } ,$ metal-ferroelectric-insulator-semiconductor (MFIS) capacitors were fabricated with $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ layers of 0, 0.25, 0.5, 1, and 2 nm under identical processing conditions (Figure S7, Supporting Information). The electrical measurements show that both ferroelectric switching and leakage behavior are highly sensitive to the $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ thickness.

For $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ thickness up to 0.5 nm, the devices exhibit well-defined ferroelectric dynamics, indicating that a moderate concentration of interfacial $\mathrm { V _ { O } }$ generated at the $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 } / \mathrm { H Z O }$ interface does not disturb the crystallinity or dipole alignment of HZO. The leakage current is also suppressed in this regime, primarily because the addition of a 0.5 nm $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ layer increases the physical barrier thickness. Owing to the high-k nature of $\operatorname { T i O } _ { 2 } ,$ this added thickness does not introduce significant voltage drop across the interlayer, allowing ferroelectric switching in the HZO film to remain essentially unaffected.

However, when the $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ thickness is increased to 1 nm or more, the ferroelectric characteristics degrade significantly and leakage current rises sharply. This degradation originates from the combined effects of (i) disruption of HZO crystallization due to the thicker $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ layer and (ii) excessive formation and redistribution of $\mathrm { V _ { O } }$ within the HZO lattice. Under these conditions, it becomes difficult to distinguish whether the observed ferroelectric suppression arises from crystallinity degradation or from over-accumulated $\mathrm { V _ { O } s }$ .

These results indicate that an optimal $\mathrm { V } _ { \mathrm { O } }$ concentration window exists near a $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ thickness of ~0.5 nm—sufficient to enable ionic-assisted switching, yet below the threshold that induces leakage or ferroelectric degradation. Based on these observations, 0.5 nm $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ was identified as the optimal thickness, providing increased interfacial $\mathrm { V } _ { \mathrm { O } }$ concentration while preserving intrinsic ferroelectric behavior. All FIDFETs in this work were therefore fabricated using this optimized condition, which enables controlled ionic activity without compromising ferroelectric stability.

Note S8: Switching mechanism overview in FeFET and FIDFET

To clarify the distinct switching mechanisms responsible for memory behavior in fabricated devices, we elaborate on the conceptual illustrations shown in Figure 2F,H, alongside dynamic MW measurements.

In the FeFET, the MW is governed solely by conventional ferroelectric switching, where ferroelectric dipoles switch between “Up” and “Down” states depending on the direction of the applied electric field. This dipole reorientation occurs rapidly—typically within ns—as it involves only small atomic displacements and no ionic migration. However, when higher voltages are applied, the FeFET enters a soft breakdown region. This leads to increased gate leakage and electron trapping near the interface. These trapped charges screen the internal field, suppressing polarization switching and limiting MW.

This limitation is clearly observed in switching speed characteristics (Figure 2I): for FeFET, the MW saturates around 100 ns regardless of further increases in pulse amplitude or width. This saturation indicates that the memory modulation is strictly limited by the intrinsic speed of FE-PGM, with no mechanism for further MW modulation beyond intrinsic dipole dynamics.

In contrast, FIDFET exhibits dual-mode switching behavior. At low voltage or short pulse widths, it operates through fast ferroelectric switching, this behavior mirrors that of FeFET, dominated by fast FE-PGM. However, as shown in Figure 2J, when the pulse amplitude exceeds a critical threshold or the duration extends beyond ~100 ns, a second switching mechanism is activated. In this regime, positively charged VOs begin to drift downward through the HZO layer, driven by the vertical electric field.

This slower ionic migration, termed hybrid PGM, unfolds over μs to ms timescales and contributes persistently to the modulation of channel potential. The cumulative redistribution of VOs near the bottom interface effectively tunes the internal field, enabling further MW expansion beyond the ferroelectric-only limit. Figure 2H illustrates this dual mechanism: initial fast dipole switching defines the memory states, while subsequent VO migration reinforces the channel potential and stabilizes the polarization.

The temporal separation between the two mechanisms allows voltage- and time-dependent control of memory characteristics: fast dipole switching enables immediate and energy-efficient response, while slow ionic migration provides deeper potential modulation and retention enhancement. Together, this dual-mode operation ensures both high-speed switching and long-term stability in FIDFET, overcoming the limitations of conventional FeFETs.

To understand the impact of switching mechanisms on long-term memory stability, we analyzed the retention characteristics of FeFET and FIDFET devices (Figure 2K,L). Conventional FeFETs rely solely on fast ferroelectric polarization switching (FE-PGM) to modulate the memory state. This switching occurs within ns, enabling high-speed operation. However, FE-PGM alone lacks intrinsic mechanisms to maintain the polarization state over long timescales. When devices are subjected to prolonged stress or repetitive cycling, injected carriers, especially electrons, may become trapped at the FE/IL interface. These trapped charges can screen the internal electric field, weaken the dipole alignment and cause gradual degradation of the MW. Since FeFETs have no additional mechanisms to restore or reinforce the internal field, they are inherently limited in retention performance.

FIDFET, in contrast, incorporates an ultrathin $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ layer and exhibits a dual-mode switching, combining fast ferroelectric dipole rotation with slow ionic migration. The controlled movement of $\mathrm { V _ { O } s }$ under electric stress gradually modulates the internal field. Rather than being detrimental, this ionic contribution helps reinforce the internal electric field, stabilize the polarization state, and sustain the MW under retention stress. Specifically, the advantages include:

1. Compensation of depolarization fields by sustaining internal polarization.   
2. Suppression of charge trapping-induces degradation, effectively mitigating the primary failure mechanism observed in FeFETs.

Consequently, FIDFET maintains a significantly wider and more stable MW for up to 10 years, clearly outperforming FeFETs in long-term retention. This improved performance arises not solely from structural differences, but from the activation of dual-mode switching the engages both ferroelectric and ionic processes.

However, this retention enhancement is not unconditional. As shown in Figure 2L, when FIDFET operates at low $V _ { \mathrm { P G M } }$ where only FE-PGM is active, the MW also gradually degrades over time, like FeFET behavior. In this regime, ionic migration is not sufficiently activated, and the device lacks the second reinforcement that stabilizes the polarization. This results in a narrow and unstable MW, despite the presence of the $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ layer.

Thus, the superior retention of FIDFET is realized only when both switching mechanisms are activated, i.e., under higher voltage and longer pulse conditions. This highlights the conditional and voltage-, temporal-dependent nature of retention enhancement. The device’s long-term stability stems from the synergistic interplay between fast ferroelectric switching and slow ionic migration, which together enable sustainable and tunable memory characteristics, capabilities not attainable in conventional single-mode FeFETs.

Note S10: Thermal and temporal modulation of VO-migration switching

To elucidate the origin of the MW expansion in FIDFET, we investigated the effects of temperature and holding time on the switching behavior. The focus was to evaluate how these external stimuli influence the onset and strength of $\mathrm { V } _ { \mathrm { O } } .$ -driven ionic switching.

To isolate this contribution, we performed a matrix of I-V measurements as a function of $V _ { \mathrm { P G M } } ,$ measurement temperature (20 to $8 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ , and holding time (0 to 20s). As shown in Figure S11, the $I { - } V$ curves are presented in a 4 X 4 matrix: each row corresponds to a fixed temperature, and each column corresponds to a different $V _ { \mathrm { P G M } }$ . Within each panel, curves plotted to increase holding time at positive voltage, enabling visualization of both thermal and temporal effects on MW behavior. This comprehensive $I { - } V$ matrix reveals that both elevated temperature and extended holding time accelerate the onset and magnified of MW expansion, consistent with the thermal activation of $\mathrm { V _ { O } }$ migration. Notably, as temperature increases from 20 to $8 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } .$ , MW expansion becomes apparent even at lower $V _ { \mathrm { P G M } } .$ , indicating earlier initiation of ionic migration. Similarly, longer holding times promote the accumulation of $\mathrm { V _ { O } s }$ near the HZO/IL interface, further enhancing electrostatic modulation and resulting in a progressive expansion of the MW. These effects are clearly reflected in the wider hysteresis loops and leftward shift in onset voltage observed across the matrix.

In summary, the temperature- and time-dependent MW expansion confirms that the hybrid-PGM in FIDFET originates from $\scriptstyle \mathrm { V o - d r i v e n }$ ionic migration. This slow but tunable mechanism complements the fast ferroelectric switching, enabling programmable, time-adaptive memory characteristics in a CMOS-compatible and scalable architecture.

# Note S11: $I _ { \mathrm { G } }$ noise analysis

Figure S14 shows the $I _ { \mathrm { G } }$ versus $V _ { \mathrm { G S } }$ curves of the FIDFET device for different states: ERS $\left( V _ { \mathrm { E R S } } = - \right.$ $5 \mathrm { V } )$ , weak PGM $( V _ { \mathrm { P G M } } = 3 . 5 \ : \mathrm { V } )$ , and strong PGM $( V _ { \mathrm { P G M } } = 4 . 5 \ : \mathrm { V } )$ . In all states, the $I _ { \mathrm { G } }$ increases with increasing $V _ { \mathrm { G S } } ,$ because a higher applied voltage raises the electric field across the gate stack. Additionally, $I _ { \mathrm { G } }$ becomes larger as $V _ { \mathrm { P G M } }$ increases. This can be explained by a decrease in tunneling electroresistance (TER) after the application of higher $V _ { \mathrm { P G M } }$ , which aligns with the mechanism observed in ferroelectric tunnel junctions (FTJs). Since the FeFET gate stack of FIDFET is analogous to an FTJ, the same reasoning applies. To analyze the physical mechanism underlying the $I _ { \mathrm { G } }$ of FIDFET, we perform LFN measurements in each of these states.

In all states, $I _ { \mathrm { G } }$ normalized power spectral density $( S _ { \mathrm { I G } } / I _ { \mathrm { G } } { } ^ { 2 } )$ decreases with increasing $I _ { \mathrm { G } }$ (Figure 3F). Interestingly, when a weak program is applied in the ERS state, the noise increases in the entire range of $I _ { \mathrm { G } } .$ However, after a strong program, the noise decreases in all $I _ { \mathrm { G } }$ regions and becomes lower than that of the ERS state. Because noise originates from fluctuations in physical quantities related to electron transport, changes in $S _ { \mathrm { I G } } / { I _ { \mathrm { G } } } ^ { 2 }$ with respect to $V _ { \mathrm { P G M } }$ confirm that the gate current switching mechanism of FIDFET depends on the magnitude of $V _ { \mathrm { P G M } }$ . Figure S14 shows (a) the $I _ { \mathrm { G } }$ versus $V _ { \mathrm { G S } }$ curves, (b) DC transient measurement results of $I _ { \mathrm { G } }$ with different states (total sampling time = 5 s, sampling $\mathrm { r a t e } = 3 2 0 0 \mathrm { H z } )$ , and (c) $S _ { \mathrm { I G } } / { I _ { \mathrm { G } } } ^ { 2 }$ versus $I _ { \mathrm { G } }$ for each state. The DC transient measurements also exhibit the same trend: the noise increases under weak programming and then decreases below the ERS-level noise under strong programming.

To investigate this behavior in more detail, Figure 3G shows $S _ { \mathrm { I G } } / { I _ { \mathrm { G } } } ^ { 2 }$ versus frequency f for different $V _ { \mathrm { P G M } }$ . In all cases, the noise follows a 1/f dependence. When $V _ { \mathrm { P G M } } { \leq } 4 . 4 \ \mathrm { V } .$ , the noise level increases as $V _ { \mathrm { P G M } }$ becomes higher, whereas for $V _ { \mathrm { P G M } } { \geq } 4 . 4 ~ \mathrm { V } .$ , increasing $V _ { \mathrm { P G M } }$ instead reduces the noise magnitude. Figure S15 shows $S _ { \mathrm { I G } } / { I _ { \mathrm { G } } } ^ { 2 }$ versus $V _ { \mathrm { P G M } }$ at a sampling frequency of 100 Hz, reaffirming that $S _ { \mathrm { I G } } / { I _ { \mathrm { G } } } ^ { 2 }$ first increases and then decreases with VPGM . The initial rise in $S _ { \mathrm { I G } } / I _ { \mathrm { G } } { } ^ { 2 }$ with an increasing VPGM can be explained by ferroelectric switching. In the low-resistance state (LRS) of an FTJ, it is known that noise arises from trap-assisted processes related to Poole-Frenkel (P-F) emission. The noise contribution from P-F emission commonly follows an expression of the form:

$$
\frac {S _ {I G}}{I _ {G} ^ {2}} = \frac {\beta N _ {T}}{E \epsilon_ {i n s} ^ {2} W L} \frac {1}{f}, (1)
$$

where $\beta$ is the fitting parameter, $N _ { \mathrm { T } }$ is the trap density, E is the electric field, ε is the permittivity of the insulator layer.[22] In gate stack of FIDFET, as VPGM increases, the net electric field in the HZO layer decreases due to ferroelectric polarization, which, according to the P-F model, [22] leads to an increase in the measured noise $( S _ { \mathrm { I G } } / { I _ { \mathrm { G } } } ^ { 2 } )$ . Figure S16 shows an energy-band diagram illustrating how the electric field changes with increasing VPGM .

However, the change in the HZO electric field alone cannot explain why the noise eventually decreases above a certain VPGM. The decrease in noise at high $V _ { \mathrm { P G M } }$ is attributed to $\mathrm { V _ { O } }$ migration. [23] When a sufficiently large VPGM is applied, positively charged $\mathrm { V _ { O } }$ sites align and form a conduction path for electrons inside the HZO. In this regime, the dominant noise source is no longer governed by P-F emission but rather by carriers flowing through this $\mathrm { V _ { O } }$ -induced conduction path. Because such conduction typically exhibits $1 / f$ noise that decreases with increasing VPGM , it agrees well with the experimental results. Moreover, Figure S14 shows a sharp increase in $I _ { \mathrm { G } }$ under strong programming, compared to weak programming, which supports the formation of a conduction path due to $\mathrm { V _ { O } }$ migration. Consequently, FIDFET exhibits coexisting switching mechanisms—ferroelectric switching at lower $V _ { \mathrm { P G M } }$ and ion (Vo) migration at higher VPGM.

To evaluate on-chip learning performance, we conducted neuromorphic system simulations using the conductance-response characteristics extracted from the fabricated FeFET and FIDFET synaptic devices. Figure S29A depicts the network architecture: two convolutional layers (3×3 kernels, stride 1) each followed by average pooling, producing 32 and 64 feature maps, respectively, and two fully connected layers (3136–512–10). Negative synaptic weights were implemented with complementary device pairs, defining each weight as the conductance difference $( \mathbf { G } ^ { + } \cdot - \mathbf { G } ^ { - } )$ between the paired elements.

Figure S29B presents the average LTP/LTD curves for 30 FeFET devices, and Figure S29C shows the corresponding curves for 30 FIDFET devices. FIDFETs exhibit superior conductance modulation characteristics compared to FeFETs. In FeFETs, identical pulses result in poor linearity due to nature of ferroelectric switching, where domain flipping occurs within a confined voltage range. Once most switchable domains have flipped at their respective coercive voltages, subsequent identical pulse has little effect on further conductance modulation. In contrast, FIDFET leverages a dual-mode switching mechanism, ferroelectric polarization combined with ionic migration of $\mathrm { V _ { O S } }$ . Unlike the binary-like domain switching in FeFETs, the gradual migration and redistribution of $\mathrm { V _ { O } s }$ under repeated identical pulses dynamically modulates the local electric field and potential landscape. This allows the channel conductance to evolve continuously, as the state remains sensitive to subtle ionic migration. As a result, FIDFET achieves more linear synaptic weight update, even under identical pulses. These results suggest that, despite the need for stronger pulse conditions, FIDFETs are more suitable as synaptic devices due to their large memory window and excellent linearity enabled by VO-mediated switching.

For system-level validation, we incorporated the measured conductance-update characteristics into our neuromorphic simulations. Each conductance-response curve was normalized to the [0, 1] range, and synaptic weight increases and decreases were modeled accordingly. The direction of each weight update was determined by the backpropagation algorithm and then mapped onto the normalized conductance-response curves to yield the final device conductance changes.

Figures S29E,F show the fitted potentiation and depression curves for the FIDFET devices, while g and h present the corresponding fits for the FeFET devices. In both cases, the fitted curves closely match the experimental data. Figure S29D presents the pattern‐classification accuracy obtained by incorporating these device characteristics into our simulations. Training was performed for 100 epochs and repeated eight times to capture statistical trends. The FeFET‐based network exhibits larger fluctuations and lower overall accuracy compared to the FIDFET ‐based network, a consequence of

the abrupt conductance jumps during FeFET depression. Poor linearity in synaptic devices degrades the accuracy of weight updates, leading to reduced learning performance and stability in neuromorphic on-chip systems. This non-ideal behavior hinders precise representation of synaptic strength, which is critical for efficient learning and inference. These results confirm that a synaptic device with a wider memory window and more linear conductance response, such as FIDFET, is better suited for stable on‐chip learning.

The introduction of an ultrathin $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ interlayer in the FIDFET structure provides multiple functional benefits that are not acguevable. The engineered $\mathrm { V _ { O } }$ dynamics at the $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 } / \mathrm { H Z O }$ interface synergistically couple with ferroelectric dipole switching, enabling the following advantages:

1. Memory window expansion and tunability (Figures 2I,J)

The FIDFET exhibits an expanded and bias-tunable memory window (MW) more than twice that of the FeFET, owing to the additional ionic contribution from controlled $\mathrm { V _ { O } }$ migration. This ferroelectric-ionic coupling enables gradual MW modulation under varying pulse amplitudes and widths while maintaining low leakage current.

2. Superior operational stability (Figures 2K,L)

The $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 }$ layer inserted alleviates interfacial charge trapping and suppresses leakage current by moderating the redistribution of $\mathrm { V _ { O S } }$ . As a result, the FIDFET maintains stable polarization and long retention characteristics over extended time, demonstrating superior reliability compared with FeFET.

3. Improved PGM/ERS linearity (Figure S29)

The combined ferroelectric-ionic switching properties in the FIDFET enable more gradual and symmetric conductivity modulation, even under identical pulse modulation, effectively overcoming the abrupt and nonlinear updates typically observed in conventional ferroelectric devices.

4. Intrinsic stochastic encoding functionality (Figures 4)

Beyond memory enhancement, the most distinguishing advantage of the FIDFET is its ability to harness RTN originating from dynamically interacting VOs at the $\mathrm { T i O } _ { 2 } / \mathrm { H Z O }$ interface. While RTN is treated as detrimental noise in traditional FeFETs, the FIDFET leverages ferroelectric-ionic coupling to achieve voltage-tunable RTN amplitude and dwell-time statistics that map naturally onto Poisson-distributed spike rates. This enables the FIDFET to serve simultaneously as a stochastic encoder and a synaptic memory element within a single $\mathrm { H f O } _ { 2 }$ platform, eliminating the need for external noise sources or additional circuits.
