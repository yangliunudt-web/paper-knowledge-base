A domain model consistent with the measured capacitance–voltage (CV) characteristics of lead zirconate titanate ~PZT! capacitors is proposed. Two variants of this model are presented and compared with experimentally measured CV data. The basic model is developed adopting a macroscopic electric field that is spatially uniform through the depth of the film. Then, this model is generalized to allow a variation of the electric field with depth and to include a physically reasonable, position-dependent domain structure. Specifically, the spatial variation of the electric field is related to dopant–ion charges. As a result of the interaction between the domain properties and the electrical doping, a position dependent permittivity is induced, and the electrical properties of the capacitors are affected. Finally, computer simulations to fit the measured CV characteristics are performed to help understand the extent of the coupling between the domain properties and the electrical doping. It is found that there is a minimum doping level below which the doping does not affect the CV characteristic. A method for determining this minimum doping level from the CV curve is presented. The analysis of observed CV data demonstrates that niobium doping is responsible for partially compensating the p-type nature of PZT thin films. For the films measured here, the minimum noticeable doping level is about $1 0 ^ { 1 8 } ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 3 }$ . It is also found that niobium doping slows the growth rate of polarization as the electric field increases, and has a tendency to increase the coercive field. © 1997 American Institute of Physics. @S0021-8979~97!03517-2#

# I. INTRODUCTION

The capacitance–voltage (CV) characteristics of a ferroelectric thin-film capacitor below the Curie point are related to the domain populations oriented variously with the applied field. A multiple-domain model is introduced for the case of a spatially uniform electric field, and shown to be capable of fitting measured CV curves. The uniform field domain model then is generalized to treat the case of spatially varying electric fields caused by charges in the films, in particular, dopant–ion charges. The uniform and nonuniform field models are compared with measured CV data of lead zirconate titanate ~PZT! capacitors, and effects of niobium doping are examined.

This work draws upon Refs. 1–3, where a fielddependent permittivity was used to model CV characteristics of $\mathrm { P b T i O } _ { 3 } , \mathrm { S r T i O } _ { 3 } ,$ , and ${ \mathrm { K T a O } } _ { 3 }$ . Kahng and $\mathrm { W e m p l e } ^ { 2 }$ and van der Berg et $a l . ^ { \bar { 3 } }$ related field-dependent permittivity models to measured CV characteristics in $\mathrm { K T a O } _ { 3 }$ and SrTiO3 Schottky diodes, respectively. However, domain structure could not have contributed to the field dependence of the permittivity in their work, which applies only above the Curie temperatures of the materials studied. Blom et al.1 introduced a position-dependent permittivity for ${ \mathrm { P b T i O } } _ { 3 }$ Schottky

diodes based on a field-dependent polarization and a spatially varying electric field. However, even though domains were present in the material under study, the field dependence of the polarization was postulated with no explanation of the role of domain structure.

Tagantsev et $a l . ^ { 4 }$ treated PZT thin films as semiconducting and studied the switching behavior of the samples for both partially and fully depleted cases. In Ref. 4, a switching model was proposed to explain the film thickness dependence of the coercive field and was found to be in qualitative agreement with their experimental results. One major concept in their switching model that is especially enlightening for this work is the proposed threshold electric fields required for domain-wall motion and reverse domain nucleation. Two important threshold electric fields were defined: the nucleation critical field, $E _ { c n }$ ; and the domain-wall motion critical field, $E _ { c m }$ . It is assumed in their switching model that once nucleation of domains of opposite polarity begins, the domains propagate through the depth of the film rapidly. In other words, $E _ { c n } \gg E _ { c m }$ . In our analysis of the multiple-domain model for the uniform field case ~Sec. III A!, a similar assumption is made implicitly, namely, $E _ { c m } = 0 .$ .

# II. EXPERIMENT

Samples studied in this work have been fabricated using the inverted mixing order sol–gel route to PZT.6 The PZT composition was 50:50 Zr:Ti with a 10% Pb excess and with niobium concentrations of 0, 1, 3, and 5 mol %. Monolithic sputtered platinum back electrodes were deposited on thermally oxidized silicon wafers using a thin Ti adhesion layer.7 Devices were made with three layers of the PZT precursor, which were separately dried before the final 650 °C oxygen– ambient firing. This resulted in coatings that were approximately 0.2 mm thick. Platinum top electrodes were patterned using a lift-off process. After top electrode deposition, an oxidizing heat treatment was performed to improve the capacitor symmetry. CV measurements were performed on 150 μm square capacitors with a 1Oo kHz small signal superimposed upon a slow dc bias. The results are shown in Fig. 1. The measurement started with a 27 V dc bias, stepped up at increments of 0.1 V until 7 V was reached, and then stepped similarly back down to 27 V. These data show a general lowering of the capacitance with increasing niobium addition.

The data collected for this series of PZT capacitors with various niobium contents will be the basis for testing the domain-related models developed below. It will be shown that asymmetries, which appear in the CV curves, can occur through coupling between the ferroelectric properties and dopant–ion induced local-field variation through the depth of

the capacitors. Fits of the experimental data support the conclusion that compensation of the p-type PZT samples occurs with the introduction of niobium.

# III. A DOMAIN MODEL FOR FERROELECTRIC FILMS

A domain model is now proposed. The basis of the model is introduced in Sec. III A using the simple case where the electric field is spatially uniform through the depth of the film. In this uniform electric-field case, two domain orientations are assumed to exist, i.e., parallel and antiparallel to the applied electric field. Domains of both orientations penetrate through the depth of the film in the uniform electric-field case. The domain structure discussed in Sec. III A for the uniform field case is similar to the observed domain structure with a 180° wall between domains of antiparallel polarization in Ref. 8. It was shown in Ref. 9 that antiparallel domains can be observed when the crystal is strained by external or internal stresses or by an external electric dc field. This supports the domain structure in Sec. III A, since the samples under study are PZT thin-film capacitors for electrical memory applications.

The simple uniform electric-field model is applied in Sec. III B to measured CV characteristics of PZT capacitors. The fit of the simple model to the measurement is reasonable. The simple case that the electric field is spatially uniform through the depth of the film is then generalized to a more complex case in Sec. III C, for which the electric field is spatially varying, specifically, due to the presence of dopant–ion charges. As will be shown shortly, the domain structure in Sec. III C with a saw-toothed interface between antiparallel domains is similar to the needle-shaped nucleation observed in Ref. 8. In Sec. III D, the spatially varying electric-field model is fitted to the measured CV characteristics of the PZT capacitors, and the fit is found to be extremely good. It is concluded that the model with the spatially varying electric field is more likely than the uniform field case.

To build a schematic domain picture for the case of a spatially nonuniform electric field, consider the P versus E relation shown in Fig. 3 that assumes a simple linear $\alpha ( E )$ . With this assumption of linear E dependence,

$$
\frac {\partial P}{\partial E} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l} 0, \quad E \leqslant E _ {a} \text {o r} E \geqslant E _ {d} \\ 2 \hat {P} / \left(E _ {d} - E _ {a}\right), \quad E _ {a} \leqslant E \leqslant E _ {d}. \end{array} \right. \tag {13}
$$

In the range of fields $E _ { a } { \leqslant } E { \leqslant } E _ { d } .$ , domains exist in both the positive and negative x directions. Using Eq. ~13! in Eqs. ~2! and ~3!, the simple field-dependent permittivity in Fig. 8 @curve ~a!# results, namely,

$$

$$

$$

Suppose that for any x inside the switching region there is an electric-field value $E ( x )$ , such that a(x) is given by $\alpha ( x ) = \alpha ( E ( x ) )$ . That is, each section of width dx within the transition layer behaves like a slice of the spatially uniform ferroelectric considered in Sec. III A. It is now assumed that the spatial dependence of the electric field, $E ( x )$ , is due to the presence of dopant–ion charges. With this origin for $E ( x )$ in mind, the permittivity model of Fig. 10 will be related to the dopant–ion charge. Poisson’s equation for a fully depleted film is

$$
\frac {\partial D}{\partial x} = \frac {\partial D}{\partial E} \frac {\partial E}{\partial x} = \epsilon (E) \frac {\partial E}{\partial x} = \rho (x) = - q N (x), \tag {16}
$$

where the space-charge density, $\rho ( x ) { = } - q N ( x )$ for p-type samples ~e.g., PZT films!. With the definition of $\epsilon ( E )$ in Eq. ~3!,

$$
\frac {\partial D}{\partial E} = \epsilon = \kappa \epsilon_ {0} + \frac {\partial P}{\partial x} \frac {\partial x}{\partial E} = \kappa \epsilon_ {0} + \frac {\partial P}{\partial x} \frac {\epsilon}{\rho}. \tag {17}
$$

Solving for e yields

$$
\epsilon = \frac {\kappa \epsilon_ {0}}{1 - (1 / \rho) \partial P / \partial x}. \tag {18}

According to Eq. ~18!, outside the switching layer where ${ \partial P } / { \partial x } { = } 0 , \ \epsilon { = } \epsilon _ { l } { = } \kappa \epsilon _ { 0 }$ .Inside the switching layer, $\partial P / \partial x$ can be approximated by the change in $P ( x )$ across the switching layer

$$

$$

The position-dependent permittivity e(x) described by Eq. ~20! is that of Fig. 10, with an explicit relation to the dopant–ion charge density.

Next, the determination of the doping profile $N ( x )$ shown in Fig. 11 is discussed. Within the framework of the field-dependent permittivity model for ferroelectrics subject to spatially nonuniform electric field, it is possible to extract the doping profile in ferroelectric thin films using an analog of the usual formula for doping profiling in Schottky barriers. A derivation of the relation between the dopant–ion charge profile, $\rho ( x )$ , and CV measurements for ferroelectrics is presented in Ref. 5. The profile is found from

$$
\rho \left(x _ {j}\right) = \frac {2}{\epsilon_ {\mathrm {e f f}} d \left(1 / C ^ {2}\right) / d V _ {a}}, \tag {23}
$$

where the effective dielectric constant, $\epsilon _ { \mathrm { e f f } } .$ , is defined as

$$
\frac {1}{\boldsymbol {\epsilon} _ {\text {e f f}}} \equiv \frac {1}{\boldsymbol {\epsilon} _ {l}} - \frac {1}{\boldsymbol {\epsilon} _ {h}}. \tag {24}
$$

$\rho ( x _ { j } )$ is the charge density of the ferroelectric film at $x$ $= x _ { j } . ~ \epsilon _ { l }$ is the saturated ~low! dielectric constant, and $\epsilon _ { h }$ is the nonsaturated ~high! dielectric constant. The profiling location, $x _ { j }$ , is at the position where the dielectric constant changes from $\epsilon _ { l }$ to $\epsilon _ { h }$ . It is found in Ref. 5 that a good approximation to $x _ { j }$ that is directly measurable is given by

$$
x _ {j} = \epsilon_ {\text {e f f}} \left(\frac {1}{C} - \frac {t}{\epsilon_ {h}}\right). \tag {25}
$$

Practically, $\epsilon _ { l , h }$ can be estimated using the measured maximum and minimum capacitance values, $C _ { \mathrm { m a x , m i n } }$ , as follows:

$$
\epsilon_ {h, l} \approx C _ {\max , \min} \times t, \tag {26}
$$

where t is the film thickness. Note that $\epsilon _ { h }$ and $\epsilon _ { l }$ calculated from Eq. ~26! will be adjusted slightly later in computer simulations in order to obtain a good fit to the measured CV characteristic. Strictly speaking, the estimated $\epsilon _ { h }$ from Eq. ~26! is a minimum for the possible values of the high dielectric constant, since it is assumed implicitly in Eq. ~26!

that the switching layer penetrates through the depth of the film at the peak capacitance, $C _ { \mathrm { m a x } }$ . However, if the switching layer is narrower than the film thickness, then the actual high dielectric constant is larger than the estimation of $\epsilon _ { h }$ in $\operatorname { E q . }$ . ~26!. Also, since the measured $C V$ characteristic never reaches its large field asymptotes because large enough voltages cause breakdown of the samples, the estimated $\epsilon _ { l }$ from Eq. ~26! is an over estimation of the actual low dielectric constant. In short, Eq. ~26! provides some ‘‘initial guesses’’ of $\epsilon _ { h }$ and $\epsilon _ { l }$ for computer simulations, but $\epsilon _ { h }$ is then slightly increased and $\epsilon _ { l }$ is slightly reduced in order to obtain a good fit to the measured $C V$ characteristic. The values of $\epsilon _ { l }$ summarized in Table I are obtained with the approach described above and will be used shortly in the doping profiling.

Equations ~23! and ~25! apply provided ~i! the ferroelectric film is fully depleted, and ~ii! that there is only one transition between $\epsilon _ { h }$ and $\epsilon _ { l }$ within the film ~i.e., film thickness,D!. It is found in our prior work18 that the first condition is likely to be satisfied for ferroelectric thin films. The second condition corresponds to the physical picture of the distribution of the permittivity through the depth of the film reported by van der Berg et al. in Ref. 3.

Based on the proposed e(E) and the approximate $N ( x )$ extracted from measurement, computer simulations have reconstructed the measured CV characteristics. Thus, a consistent formulation of e(E), e(x), r(x), and the measured CV characteristic within the framework of the nonuniform electric-field assumption has been demonstrated.

# IV. DISCUSSION

It has been shown that domain models for the uniform field case ~Sec. III A case I! and for the spatially varying field case ~Sec. III C case II! are both consistent with measured CV characteristics of PZT capacitors. In the domain model for the case of a uniform electric field discussed in Sec. III C, domains both parallel and antiparallel to the ap-

plied field penetrate through the depth of the film. When the electric field becomes spatially varying due to the presence of dopant–ion charges, the domain model introduced for the uniform field case was generalized into a domain structure where an interface ~idealized as a sawtooth! exists between domains of antiparallel polarizations. Domains with either orientation do not penetrate entirely through the depth of the film in the case of a spatially varying field. Instead, domains parallel to the applied field exist on one side of the film and antiparallel domains on the other side, with a transition region that is composed of a mixture of both domain orientations ~Fig. 9!.

Since both domain models fit the measured CV data reasonably well, the question arises: which domain model is more representative of reality? As mentioned, the domain model for the case of a uniform electric field generalizes to a domain structure with a transition region ~idealized as a sawtooth! when the electric field becomes spatially varying. Hence, in order to verify which domain structure applies better, the spatial variation of the electric field needs to be established, for example, by verifying the presence of dopant–ion charges.

Based on the analysis in Sec. III C, the presence of dopant–ion charge can be deduced from the CV measurements. A possible confusion arises: if a CV curve of a sample known to be undoped were to be measured, where the electric field is known to be uniform, but nonetheless the Schottky profiling formula Eq. ~23! were applied inadvisably, an apparent doping profile would be extracted, even though the dopant concentration actually is known to be zero. Uncritical acceptance of this incorrect profile would lead to incorrect conclusions about the domain structure.

A procedure is now described to avoid such errors. # V. SUMMARY

A domain model has been proposed and related to the measured CV characteristics of PZT capacitors with various niobium doping levels. The analysis has been compared with measurements. For zero dopant–ion charge, the model has a spatially uniform electric field and a simple multiple-domain structure with two domain orientations ~parallel and antiparallel to the applied field!. For the switching range of fields, domains of both orientations are assumed to penetrate throughout the depth of the film. On this basis, a volume

growth rate of polarization, as a function of electric field α(E) has been extracted from the measured CV characteristic.   
The zero-dopant model then was generalized for the case of a spatially varying electric field. The extended model accounts for a nonuniform electric field caused by dopant–ion charges. Based on the nonuniform electric-field model, a position-dependent permittivity has been deduced and doping profiling has been performed based on the permittivity model. The profiling supports the reported compensation of the p-type PZT samples by the n-type niobium doping. Based on the proposed permittivity model and the approximate doping profiles extracted from the CV measurement, the measured CV characteristics of PZT capacitors with various niobium doping levels have been predicted.   
Comparison of CV measurements made on samples with various niobium content suggests that niobium doping slightly slows the volume growth of polarization ~as shown by the decrease in d a/dE and $\epsilon _ { h }$ in Tables I and II! with the electric field and that niobium is a compensating donor dopant in PZT ~as shown in Fig. 11!. Further, a methodology is proposed to identify the minimum electrical doping level above which doping profile information can be reliably extracted. This minimum doping level can be estimated using the measured CV curve and is approximately $1 0 ^ { 1 8 } ~ \mathrm { c m } ^ { - 3 }$ for measurements in this work.   
Although the models used are simplified, a consistent framework has been presented relating profiling to CV measurements and domain structure. This framework provides a

basis for further experimental and theoretical work on thinfilm ferroelectrics.
